## Chapter Text
Wren gathered the copied sheets with both hands, pressing them flat against her chest to stop their rustling. The docket was already folded inside her coat, a hard rectangle against her ribs. She did not cap her pen—there was no time—and left it rolling on the desk as she blew out the lamp.

The corridor was dark. She moved by memory, one hand on the wall, past the assessors' closed doors, past the framed portrait of the first Harbor Master that no one ever looked at. The back stair was narrower than the main, used by the charwomen and the watchman on his rounds. She took it downward, her boots finding the worn center of each step where a thousand boots had gone before.

The door at the bottom opened onto the alley behind the Customs House, where the fish-gut carts came in the morning. The smell hit her first, then the cold. The harbor fog had rolled in while she worked, and the lamplight from the quay was only a dull smear somewhere to her left. Wren pulled her collar up and walked, not running—running in fog drew eyes—and her hand kept moving to her coat to check that the docket was still there, that the folded sheets had not slipped.

She had eight quarters copied. Eight out of seventy-six. Enough to see the pattern, not enough to prove it to anyone who did not already know. The names of six clerks besides her own, each one a junior who had sat where she sat and copied the line and thought nothing of it. She tried to remember their faces and could not. The Customs House churned through clerks the way the harbor churned through tide.

Her lodging was two streets up from the water, above a chandlery that closed at dusk. She climbed the stairs with her boots in her hand so the landlady would not hear, though the landlady was deaf in one ear and pretended to be deaf in the other. The room was cold, the fire long dead. Wren lit her own lamp from the tinderbox and spread the sheets on the table by the window.

The fog moved past the glass like something breathing. She weighted the corners with her inkwell, her pen-case, the little stone her father had given her years ago that was supposed to bring a sailor home. The sheets made a ragged row. She had not copied the amounts—only the dates, the ship names, the clerk signatures, the seal notations. Even so, the pattern was unmistakable. Every quarter. The same hand placing the heron seal. The same ship that had been at the bottom of the sea since before Wren learned to write.

She took the docket from her coat and smoothed it on the table beside the sheets. The weigher's capitals. The looping _Cask_. The pencilled line underneath: _Tell O. the lamp is lit._

Someone knocked at the door below, two streets away, and Wren's hand went to her throat. But it was only a drunkard looking for the chandler's wife, or a sailor who had mistaken the door. The knock did not repeat. She sat with her palm flat on the copied sheets, feeling her own pulse through the paper, and listened until the footsteps went away.

Tomorrow night the ship would come in. Varga had said so. Wren had a day to decide what she was going to do with what she knew, and she did not have enough to do any of the things that felt like justice. She had enough to understand that she was part of it now—that her own hand, her own slanted sevens, had helped keep the lie alive for two years without her knowing.

The harbor bell rang the half-tide, muffled by fog. Wren left the sheets on the table and lay down on her bed in her coat, the docket still in the pocket, and watched the lamp flame shrink in the glass until it was no larger than the heron seal she saw when she closed her eyes.
