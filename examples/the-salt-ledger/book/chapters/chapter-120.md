## Chapter Text
Wren did not move her hands from the desk edge. The confession had brought them to a shared ledger, two columns of complicity that balanced too neatly, but a ledger was only as useful as what it explained. She wanted the rest of the account. Not the origin story, polished by nineteen years of private rehearsal, but the middle — the long years between the first docket and tonight, the accumulated weight of quarterly silence.

"Tell me the rest," she said. "Not how it began. What it became."

Varga looked at her for a long moment, as if measuring whether Wren had earned the continuation. Then she reached into her coat pocket and withdrew a small object: a docket blank, unmarked, folded twice. She set it on the blotter between them, not a document but a placeholder, something to hold her hands while she spoke.

"The first year," Varga said, "I pressed every seal myself. I wrote every entry. I told myself I was being careful, controlling the record, but the truth was simpler: I did not trust anyone else to carry the weight. The second year, I had a clerk — Thom, his name was, a nervous boy with beautiful copperplate. I gave him the Margery Lane docket and stood at his shoulder while he copied it. He did not ask. They never asked."

She unfolded the blank docket, smoothed it flat. "By the fifth year, I no longer stood at their shoulders. I left the docket on the tithe desk with the others, and whoever was on duty copied it the way they copied everything else. By the tenth year, I stopped preparing the docket myself. It simply appeared, every quarter, in the box from the weighing shed. Cask's hand, or someone's hand, and the tithe paid in advance. I only pressed the seal."

Wren watched her fingers move across the blank paper, tracing a pattern that was not there. "And the clerks?"

"Fourteen of them, in nineteen years." Varga's voice had grown flatter, the tone she used when reading figures aloud. "Some copied for one quarter and moved on. Some stayed two years, three. One — Ilona, a girl from the fish market — she worked the tithe desk for five years, copied the Margery Lane twenty times, and never once looked at the name. She died last winter, a fever. I pressed the seal at her funeral docket too."

The harbor bell rang, closer now, and Varga paused until the sound faded. "You want to know what it became. It became habit. It became a line in a column that always balanced, and after enough years, a balanced column feels like virtue. The eleven men on the plaque became a story I told myself — not that they were gone, but that they were *accounted for*. The triple tithe paid for their silence. The seal made it official. The balance made it right."

She stopped. Her fingers stilled on the blank docket, and when she looked up, her eyes held the exhaustion of someone who had finished a long ledger only to find the total was not what she had expected.

"But it was never right," Wren said.

"No." The word was barely audible. "It was never right. I knew that by the twelfth quarter, when I found myself counting the strokes of the harbor bell on the night the docket arrived, matching them to the eleven names I had memorized from the plaque. I knew it by the twentieth quarter, when I started leaving the ledger room during the copying, unable to watch another clerk write the name without seeing the seawater in Cask's hair. By the fortieth quarter, I had stopped knowing what I felt. By the sixtieth, I had stopped feeling."

The fog pressed against the window, a grey weight that seemed to lean into the room, listening. Wren sat with her hands still on the desk edge and understood that she was hearing something worse than a crime: she was hearing a crime that had become a job, a sin that had been filed and indexed and balanced until it was indistinguishable from duty.

"And Cask?" she asked. "What became of him?"

Varga's jaw tightened. "I do not know. I have not seen him since the night he stood on my threshold. The dockets come through channels I never traced — the weighing shed, the Tidewardens, perhaps the cellar boxes themselves. The money is real. The signature is his, or a good imitation. I press the seal, I balance the account, and I do not ask what man or ghost writes the name."

She looked at Wren, and for the first time, something like appeal moved in her face. "That is the truth of what it became, Miss Adair. Not a conspiracy. Not a network of guilty men. Only one woman who agreed to a lie, and then agreed again, and again, until the lie became the structure her days were built on. The lamp at berth nine burns whether I believe in it or not. The docket arrives whether I want it or not. The only choice I have made in nineteen years is to keep pressing the seal, because stopping would mean building a new life around the absence of something I no longer know how to want."

The stove had gone cold. The lamplight flickered, low on oil, turning the ledger room into a smaller space than it was, a cell of yellow flame and grey fog. Wren sat in the fading light and felt the full weight of the account settle over her: nineteen years, seventy-six quarters, fourteen clerks, eleven names on a plaque, one woman who had traded her silence for balance and then forgotten how to want anything else.

She could ask Varga to name the eleven men, to recite them like a catechism and feel what nineteen years of quarterly filing had done to memory and grief. She could ask what would happen if Varga simply stopped — if the seal went unpressed, the account unbalanced, the lamp at berth nine left to burn or gutter without her participation. Or she could stand and walk to the door, and see whether Varga would follow her into the fog, away from the ledger room where confession had become its own form of continuation.
