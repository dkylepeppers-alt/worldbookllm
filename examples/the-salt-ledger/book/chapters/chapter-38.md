## Chapter Text
Wren stepped into the lamplight.

The flame caught her first, the edge of her coat, her hands empty at her sides. She had walked out of the fog as if the quay belonged to clerks after midnight, as if berth nine were just another desk in a building that happened to float. The light was warm on her face after the cold of the warehouse wall. She felt exposed down to the bone, the roll of paper inside her coat suddenly obvious as a deformity, her own breathing too loud in the stillness.

The woman on the boat saw her immediately. Wren had not made a sound, but the keeper's head turned as if the lamplight itself had spoken, announcing a second shape where only one should stand. She was older than Wren had thought, or the fog had made her seem younger from a distance — fifty, perhaps, with the broad hands of someone who had rowed through more winters than Wren had lived. Her oilskins were mended at the shoulder with a different shade of canvas, the repair old enough to have softened at the edges.

"You're not Customs," the woman said. Not a question, but not hostile either. A classification, the way a weigher might name a fish she had not expected in the net.

"I'm a clerk." Wren's voice came out level, the same tone she used when Varga asked her to read a column aloud. "I work the tithe desk."

The woman's hands stayed on the gunwale. She did not look toward the warehouse or the fog behind Wren, did not search for companions. Either she knew Wren was alone, or she had stopped caring who else might come.

"The tithe desk." A pause. "Then you've written the name."

Wren felt the words land. Not *copied* — *written*. The woman knew the distinction. She knew that someone sat in the ledger room and transferred the docket's lines into the Salt Ledger, that the hand changed every quarter but the name stayed the same. She knew that Wren's hand, with its slanted sevens and its crossed double-l, had carried the lie forward like all the others.

"I've written it," Wren said. "Tonight I found the lamp."

The woman looked at the flame between them, the small steady dish of light that had burned for nineteen years without appearing in any ledger. "The lamp finds you," she said. "Or it doesn't. You didn't come here because of the light."

"I came because of the heron seal."

Something shifted in the woman's face — not recognition, but a settling, as if a weight she had carried on one shoulder had finally been allowed to drop. She stepped onto the quay, the boat rocking behind her, and stood at her full height, which was not much taller than Wren but seemed to occupy more space, more gravity, more of the night.

"Master Assessor Varga," she said. "She's the heron."

"Yes."

"And she knows you're here."

Wren thought of the cellar, Varga's voice very quiet: *There is a ship coming in on tomorrow night's tide.* She thought of the footsteps that had paused outside the ledger room door, the watchman who had listened and moved on.

"She knows I found the entries," Wren said. "She doesn't know I'm here."

The woman studied her for a long moment. The lamp burned between them, its flame steady as a held breath, and Wren understood that she was being measured against something — not the law, which had no voice at berth nine, but the patience of someone who had kept a secret through fog and winter and the indifference of nineteen years of clerks.

"I'm Maren," the woman said at last. "I keep the boats. I keep the lamp. I don't keep the ledger — that's Varga's work, and the clerks who write what they're told." She tilted her head, assessing. "You're the first clerk who came to see what the writing meant."

"I'm the first who asked."

"Asking's different from coming." Maren's eyes went to Wren's coat, the place where the roll of paper pressed against the fabric. "You've got something in there. More than curiosity."

Wren did not reach for the roll. To touch it now would be to declare herself, to transform from a clerk who had wandered into the fog into someone who had prepared for this meeting. She stood in the lamplight with Maren watching her, the boat creaking at its mooring, the tide whispering against the stone, and knew that the next thing she did would decide whether she left berth nine as a witness or as something else entirely.

The fog moved around them, indifferent to human names. The lamp burned on, saying without words that the arrangement held, that someone was expected, that the night was not yet finished with either of them.
