## Chapter Text
Wren crouched beside the lamp. The dish was warm against her palm, the oil barely rippling in its reservoir. She had expected something heavier, something that carried the weight of nineteen years, but it was only tin and cheap glass, the kind of lamp a weigher might use to read a docket in the dark. The wick was trimmed short, burning with a parsimony that suggested someone tended it regularly, someone who understood how long a quarter-inch of wick lasted in harbor fog.

She lifted the dish by its cool rim and set it down again three feet closer to the water, where the fog would catch the flame differently and the boat would see it from a changed angle. The light shifted. It caught the hull's planking instead of the quay, gilding the waterline where barnacles fretted the wood, and for a moment the boat looked like something emerging rather than something moored.

Then a board creaked, not the tide's random movement but a deliberate step, and a figure rose from the well where the mast stepped.

"You're early."

The voice was a woman's, low and carrying, with the flattened vowels of the north quay. Wren could see nothing but a silhouette against the fog, a shape in oilskins with the breadth of shoulders that came from pulling oars through the Teeth. The figure did not move to the gunwale immediately. She stood in the well, watching the repositioned lamp, and Wren understood that she had disturbed a ritual whose steps were older than the Customs House.

"The lamp's moved," the woman said. Not a question. She came to the rail with the easy balance of someone who had never known dry ground, her hands resting on the gunwale, her face still hidden. "Who told you to move the lamp?"

Wren's fingers found the roll of paper inside her coat, the cord still tied with the knot she had learned as a girl, binding her father's nets. She had the complete record. She had the docket with its pencilled message. She had moved the signal, and now someone who knew the old arrangement was asking who had given her permission.

The woman waited. Her hands did not leave the gunwale. Wren thought of the watchman's rounds, of Varga's footsteps in the cellar, of the seventy-six entries in her coat and the eleven names on the harbor plaque. Someone had to speak first, and whoever did would decide what this night became.
