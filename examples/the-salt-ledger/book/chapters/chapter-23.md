## Chapter Text
Wren walked on.

The quay narrowed past the fish market, the cobbles giving way to older stone, slick with kelp and the residue of a thousand tides. The fog pressed close enough to taste, salt and cold and something else — lamp oil, perhaps, or the particular mustiness of a hull that had been in the water too long. She kept one hand on the warehouse wall to her left, her fingers finding the gaps between the granite blocks, her right arm clamped against the roll of paper inside her coat.

The sound of the boat grew sharper: not the creak of a vessel at rest but the purposeful small noises of someone aboard. A bucket knocked against a gunwale. A line ran through a block and was checked. Wren stopped where the wall ended and the open quay began, the last of the warehouse's shelter falling away from her shoulder like a dropped cloak.

Ahead, through the fog, she saw the light.

It was not the watchman's lantern, which moved and swung and cast a yellow bloom against the grey. This light was fixed, low, a single steady flame burning somewhere at the level of the quay itself. She thought of the pencilled message on the docket — _Tell O. the lamp is lit_ — and her breath caught in a way that had nothing to do with the cold.

Berth nine.

She had never been this far down the north quay. There was no reason for a clerk to come here, no cargo to inspect, no ledger to balance. The Tidewardens kept their boats at the quay's end, and the Customs House left them to it, a quiet territorial understanding older than the building where she worked. The lamp burned where no lamp should need to burn, at a berth where no ship ever came in.

Wren took three more steps. The fog thinned just enough to show her the shape of a small boat moored to the bollards, a Tidewarden craft by the cut of it, low in the water with a single mast stepped forward. The lamp sat on the quay beside it, not hung from a pole or housed in a lantern but set on the stone itself, a simple dish of oil with a wick, burning with a flame so steady it might have been tended moments ago.

There was no one visible. The boat rocked slightly, and Wren saw that its oars were in the locks, the painter made fast with a knot that could be slipped in a hurry. Ready. Waiting. The lamp was not a beacon for ships coming in from the Teeth — it was too low, too small, too easily missed in any weather but fog. It was a signal for someone already on the water, or for someone watching from the quay, a private language written in flame and darkness.

She thought of Varga in the cellar, her voice very quiet: _There is a ship coming in on tomorrow night's tide._ The lamp was lit tonight. The lamp had been lit, perhaps, for nineteen years, while clerks like her copied the line and thought nothing of it.

The fog shifted. A board creaked on the boat, and Wren froze, but it was only the tide moving through the hull, the ordinary respiration of a vessel at mooring. She was alone on the quay with the lamp and the boat and the complete record heavy against her ribs, seventy-six quarters of a truth that had kept itself burning in the dark.

The harbor bell rang once, distant, marking some hour she had lost track of. The flame did not flicker. Wren stood at the edge of berth nine with the fog around her and the tide whispering against the stone, and understood that she had found the living heart of the lie — not in the ledger room, not in the cellar boxes, but here, where the water met the quay and a single lamp said that someone was expected.
