## Chapter Text
The chair scraped against the floorboards, a small sound that seemed too loud in the room's held breath. Wren sat. The desk edge pressed against her ribs, familiar and strange at once—she had spent three years in this chair, copying dockets, checking columns, believing that the reason for every figure was written down somewhere if you were willing to look.

Varga watched her settle. The heron ring turned once, twice, catching the lamp flame. Then she folded her hands on the blotter, the way she did when preparing to read a ledger aloud, and began.

"His name was Orrin Cask when I knew him first." Her voice was quiet, measured, each word placed like a weight on a scale. "He came to my door in the small hours, three days after the squall. The Teeth had kept the Margery Lane, or so the harbor believed. Eleven men, the plaque says. Eleven men and a hold full of copper. But Cask was not among them. He had swum to the pilot boat in the dark, and the pilot boat had not come back to Harrowmere."

Wren felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it did when the tide turned beneath the pilings. She thought of Maren on the quay, of the uncle who had died guiding the ship, of the lamp that burned where no lamp needed to burn.

"He stood on my threshold with seawater still in his hair," Varga continued. "Salt had dried white on his collar. He asked me if I wanted the harbor to know that the Margery Lane had not sunk with all hands. He asked me if I wanted to explain why a man who was supposed to be dead had swum away from eleven men who weren't so fortunate."

The stove ticked. Outside, the fog pressed against the glass, erasing the harbor, erasing the world beyond this room and its two occupants.

"What did you say?" Wren asked.

Varga's eyes met hers. "I said nothing. I let him stand there dripping on my mat until he told me what he wanted. He wanted the Margery Lane to continue paying its salt tithe. He wanted the ledger to show a ship that still sailed, a cargo that still moved, a harbor account that still balanced. In exchange, he would pay. Double the tithe, then triple. He would pay enough to keep the question from ever being worth asking."

"And you agreed."

"I agreed." Varga's hands unfolded, spread flat on the blotter, the heron ring facing Wren like a seal pressed in grey wax. "I was not Master Assessor then. I was a woman with a small room above the chandlery and a reputation for keeping accounts that balanced. I had watched clerks come and go, watched the harbor swallow their careful work and give nothing back. Cask offered me a ledger that would never need explaining. A column that added up because the money was real, even if the ship was not."

She paused. The silence stretched, thin and trembling, between them.

"The first docket," Wren said. "The one with your seal."

"I pressed it myself. Fresh wax, the ring my mother had left me, the heron standing on one leg. I wrote the entry in my own hand because there was no clerk I trusted with it yet. I made the Margery Lane pay copper, forty hundredweight, berth nine. I made it real on the page. And then I waited to see if anyone would look closely enough to see the ghost."

Varga's voice had not changed—still measured, still controlled—but something moved beneath it, a current Wren had never heard before. Not guilt, exactly. Something older and more patient, the quality of a woman who had made a choice in a single night and had spent nineteen years discovering what that choice meant.

"No one looked," Wren said. It was not a question.

"No one looked. The tithe desk changed hands, the clerks copied what was put before them, the harbor account balanced and the town grew rich enough not to ask questions about one more ship among hundreds. And Cask paid, every quarter, through channels I never asked about. The money was real. The ship was not. The balance held."

She stopped. Her hands remained flat on the blotter, the heron ring steady in the lamplight. Wren looked at her—really looked—and saw what nineteen years of balance had cost: the grey beneath the eyes, the slight tremor in the fingers that had pressed seventy-six seals, the way her shoulders held their stiffness not from pride but from the long labor of carrying something alone.

"Why me?" Wren asked. "Why tell me this now?"

Varga's mouth tightened, a minute adjustment. "Because you came back without the evidence. Because you walked to berth nine and saw the lamp and returned with empty hands. Because—" She stopped. The heron ring turned once more, slowly. "Because I have been waiting nineteen years for someone to ask the right question, and I find I am tired of answering the wrong ones to myself."

The harbor bell rang, distant and slow, counting an hour that belonged to neither night nor morning. Wren sat in her own chair, at her own desk, and felt the story settle around her like sediment in still water. She had asked for the truth, and Varga had given her a man with seawater in his hair, a ring pressed in wax, a ledger that balanced because someone had decided long ago that balance was worth more than honesty.

But understanding was not the same as forgiveness. And knowing how the arrangement began did not tell her what it had become, or what would happen when tomorrow night's tide brought whatever it brought to berth nine.

Varga waited. The lamplight held them both in its small circle, and beyond it the fog moved, patient and indifferent, carrying the smell of the sea and the weight of nineteen years that had finally found their voice.
