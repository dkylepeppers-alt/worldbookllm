## Chapter Text
Wren stepped backward into the fog.

Not quickly — the same unhurried pace Varga used on stairs, the step that did not announce itself. One pace, then another, the lamplight withdrawing from her collar and her hands until she was only dark shape among darker shapes, the warehouse wall rough against her shoulder blades. The roll of paper no longer pressed against her ribs. She felt the absence like a missing tooth, a space where something had been that she had chosen to give away.

She found her new vantage twenty paces down, where a stack of salt-cod barrels made a gap just wide enough to see between. The fog had thinned here, or perhaps she had learned to read it, the grey shifting in layers that revealed and concealed. Through the gap she could see the lamp, the boat, the woman's shape still standing with her back to the quay. Maren — she would call her that, though the name had never been spoken aloud between them. The name felt borrowed, like everything else Wren had taken tonight.

The woman did not move for a long time. The lamp burned at her feet, steady, patient, the same quality of flame Wren had watched for hours. Then she turned, not toward the warehouse where Wren hid but toward the water, and her shoulders dropped slightly — not relief, but the settling of a weight accepted. She had found the roll. She had measured it. Now she would decide what it meant to her vigil.

Wren watched her kneel. The motion was practiced, the knee finding the stone without search, the hand steady as it touched the roll's edge. She did not unroll it. She only held it for a moment, feeling the weight of copied sheets and docket, the seventy-six quarters of a lie that had kept itself burning in the dark. Then she set it down precisely where Wren had left it, at the margin of the lamplight, neither in shadow nor fully illuminated. A message in placement, as deliberate as the lamp itself.

She stood and checked the boat again — the painter, the oars, the small shift of hull on the turning tide. Her movements were economical, the habits of someone who had done this long enough that the ritual had become indistinguishable from breathing. Wren thought of the clerks whose names she had copied, their hands moving across the same page year after year, each one thinking nothing of it. Maren was not like them. Maren knew what the lamp meant. The question was whether she knew what the roll meant, or whether she would need to ask someone else.

The harbor bell rang once, distant, muffled by fog. Maren's head lifted at the sound, not startled but attentive, the way a pilot listens for a change in the wind. She looked toward the Teeth, though the Teeth were invisible, swallowed by grey and distance. Then she looked back at the roll, and for a moment Wren saw her face clearly in the lamplight — not young, not old, the kind of face that harbor work made, weathered into patience.

She did not take the roll with her when she stepped back onto the boat. She left it where it lay, at the edge of the light, and settled onto the thwart with her own lamp turned low, waiting. The message was clear: the roll was not hers to claim, or not yet. It was an offering left at a shrine, and the shrine-keeper would tend it until the proper recipient came.

Wren's legs ached from crouching. The fog found every gap in her clothing, the damp working its way to her skin. She had no leverage now — no evidence to show, no record to threaten with, only her own eyes and what they had seen. The complete account of the fraud sat at berth nine, entrusted to a woman whose name she was not certain of, in a place where no Customs House clerk had ever been meant to stand.

She thought of Varga's voice in the cellar, very quiet: *You may think about what happens if you don't.* Wren had thought. She had copied and walked and watched and left behind the only proof she had gathered. Now she was a clerk with empty hands and a full memory, standing in fog while a woman she did not know tended a lamp that should not burn, and the tide turned beneath the quay without regard for what any of them decided.

The fog thickened again, swallowing the boat, swallowing Maren's lowered lamp until only the shore flame remained visible, a single steady point in the grey. Wren watched it and understood that she had crossed a line more final than any ledger entry. She had shown herself, however indirectly, to the living mechanism of the lie. She could not unsee what she had seen. She could not pretend to be one of the clerks who copied the line and thought nothing of it.

The question was what she had become instead.
