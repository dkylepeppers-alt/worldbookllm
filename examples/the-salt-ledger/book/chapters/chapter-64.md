## Chapter Text
Wren looked at the lamp burning between them, the small steady flame that had outlasted clerks and assessors and perhaps even the ship it summoned.

"Keep it unlit tomorrow," she said.

Maren did not answer immediately. She turned to face the water, her oilskins creaking, and the silence stretched long enough that Wren thought she had refused without words. Then Maren reached down and touched the lamp's wick with one scarred finger, not extinguishing it, only tracing the shape of the flame where it stood.

"Once," she said, "there was a storm. Not a squall like the one that took the Margery Lane—a winter storm, the kind that comes down from the north and drives the tide higher than the quay. I couldn't get to the lamp. The water was over the stones, and I held to the warehouse door and watched it drown."

Her voice had gone rougher, not with the salt that usually roughened it but with something older, kept polished by long handling.

"I thought that was the end. I stood in the doorway with the water at my knees and I let myself think it. No flame, no signal, no arrangement. Just the storm doing what the sea does to things that burn too long in one place."

She withdrew her finger from the wick and straightened, her eyes still on the black water beyond the boat.

"The ship came anyway. I heard the oars before I saw her—heard the particular dip and lift of a hull that knows these waters better than the pilots who chart them. She found the harbor mouth by the bell, by the shape of the Teeth against the current, by memory and by need. The lamp was a courtesy, Wren. It was never the reason she came."

Wren felt the words settle, heavy and unwelcome. She had imagined the flame as the heart of the lie, the single point that could be extinguished to make the rest collapse. But Maren was telling her that the arrangement ran deeper than courtesy, that nineteen years of patience had carved a channel through the harbor that needed no light to follow.

"You wished for it," Wren said. It was not a question.

Maren's shoulders moved, a breath or a shrug or something between them. "I have wished for many things. That the storm would take the lamp and I would be absolved. That someone would come and tell me to stop, and I would have a reason that wasn't my own wanting." She turned back, and her pale eyes were wet in the lamplight, though her voice held steady. "You are the first clerk to ask. You are the first person in nineteen years to stand here and suggest that darkness might be the answer."

The fog moved between them, carrying the smell of the fish market and the deeper salt of the open harbor. The boat knocked once against its fenders, a small sound that seemed loud in the stillness.

"But I cannot promise you," Maren continued. "I light the lamp because it is what I do. Because someone on the other side still believes it matters, and because I do not know what happens to a ship that comes expecting a signal and finds only the dark and a clerk with a roll of copied paper. I do not know if she would turn back, or if she would come ashore anyway, or if the men who guide her would decide that the absence of light means the arrangement is ended and all debts are called due."

She stepped closer, close enough that Wren could see the lines at the corners of her eyes, the map of a face that had watched this same flame through more seasons than Wren had lived.

"What I know is this: the lamp is easier to drown than the reason it burns. And if you want tomorrow to be different, you will need more than my silence. You will need a reason that holds against the tide, and a place to stand when the ship comes in anyway."

The harbor bell began to ring, distant and slow, measuring the hour toward morning. The flame between them did not flicker. Wren stood with empty hands and the weight of seventy-six quarters in her memory, understanding that she had found not the keeper of a lie but its first willing prisoner—and that Maren's grief was a door that opened both ways.
