## Chapter Text
Wren did not sit. She stood in the doorway with the fog still clinging to her coat, the lamplight cutting across her boots and finding the worn leather where the quay stones had pressed. Varga's hands rested on the desk, the heron ring catching the flame, and for a moment the two women were held in the amber stillness of the ledger room, surrounded by nineteen years of balanced columns.

"The eleven men," Wren said.

Varga's fingers moved, a slight adjustment, the ring turning so that the heron faced away from the light. "Eleven men," she repeated, and her voice had changed. The measured authority was still there, but beneath it something else had surfaced, a roughness like rope frayed by long use. "Eleven men and a hold full of copper. The plaque says they were lost. The plaque does not say that they were in a hurry because Cask was in a hurry, because the copper was smelted hot and the buyer was waiting in Port Moray, because the Tidewarden who warned them about the squall was waved off with a handful of coin."

She stopped. The pen on the blotter lay aligned with the desk edge, and she looked at it as if it were a hand she could not quite remember how to hold.

"I counted them," Varga said. "The first quarter, I wrote the name and I pressed the seal and I counted them. Corvin Hark. Emil Voss. The boy whose name I have forgotten because he was only there to replace his brother's broken arm. I counted them because the docket came in with Cask's signature and the copper was listed and the tithe was triple, and I understood that I was being paid to make the dead convenient."

The stove ticked in the corner, a cooling contraction of metal. Wren stood motionless, the fog dripping from her collar onto her neck, and watched Varga's face in the lamplight. The tiredness was visible now, not the sleeplessness of a single night but the accumulation of nights, the arithmetic of a woman who had balanced a harbor account against eleven lives and found the columns still wanting.

"They drowned," Varga continued. "Not all at once—the Teeth don't keep a ship that cleanly. The hull broke on the second reef and the hold flooded with copper and seawater, and some of them lived long enough to climb the foremast. Cask lived longest. He clung to a spar through the night and washed up on the mudflats below the north quay, and when he came to my door three days later he still had brine in his hair and the copper stain on his hands where he had tried to bail with his bare palms."

She looked up at Wren then, and her eyes were not the eyes of a mentor or an antagonist but simply the eyes of someone who had been present at the beginning of a long and patient lie.

"I could have reported him. The ship was lost, the men were dead, the copper was on the bottom of the sea. The harbor account would have closed the Margery Lane's file and that would have been the end of it—eleven names on a plaque, a memorial in the chapel, the ordinary grief of a port that loses men every winter. But Cask offered me triple tithe. He offered me the price of the copper, paid quarterly, to keep the Margery Lane alive in the ledger so that he could keep using her name. And I took it because the harbor was in debt that year, because the Customs House needed the revenue, because—"

She stopped again. The silence filled the room like fog, pressing against the window glass, making the lamplight seem smaller and more fragile.

"Because I told myself that balance was safer than truth. That if the columns added up, the reason could be written down later. That someone else would ask, eventually, and I would not have to be the one who decided."

Varga's hand closed on the pen, not writing, only holding it. "Nineteen years, Miss Adair. I have pressed the heron seal two hundred and twenty-eight times for a ship that does not exist, and every time I have counted eleven men. Every time I have told myself that the next quarter would be the last, that Cask would stop paying, that the arrangement would end of its own weight. And every quarter the docket arrived, and the lamp was lit, and the harbor account balanced, and I was still the woman who chose to let the dead pay for their own burial."

The pen in her hand trembled, barely, a motion so slight that Wren might have imagined it. But she did not imagine the wetness in Varga's eyes, the sheen that the lamplight caught and held, the grief of someone who had learned to count in quarters what should have been counted once, in grief, and released.

"You asked," Varga said. "You are the first clerk in nineteen years who asked. And I find I am tired of standing, and I am tired of counting, and I do not know anymore whether I want you to stop or whether I want you to make me stop."

The harbor bell rang, distant and slow, counting an hour that belonged to neither of them. Wren stood in the doorway with the fog at her back and the warmth of the ledger room at her face, and understood that the confrontation had become something else—not an interrogation, not a confession, but a reckoning between two women who had both learned to see what others skimmed, and who must now decide what seeing was worth.
