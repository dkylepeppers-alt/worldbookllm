## Beat

Wren returns to the Customs House through the fog without the copied record, finding Varga waiting in the ledger room, and their confrontation shifts from evidence to witness—what Wren saw at berth nine against what Varga has always known.

## Choices

- **Sit down** (a new beat): acceptance — Wren accepts Varga's framing and hears what she is willing to tell, at the cost of being drawn deeper into her mentor's version of events
- **Ask what happened to the eleven men** (a new beat): confrontation — Wren redirects Varga's invitation toward the human cost that the ledger does not record
- **Leave and find the watchman** (a new beat): defiance — Wren walks away from Varga's private reckoning and seeks an official witness, ending the possibility of secret understanding

## Chapter Text
Wren left the roll where it lay, a grey cylinder at the edge of lamplight, and stepped backward into the fog. The woman in oilskins did not turn. The harbor bell rang once, distant, and Wren was already moving, her boots finding the gaps between cobbles, her arms empty of everything but the cold.

The fog had thickened since she came down the quay. She kept the warehouse wall to her left, counting doorways by memory: the cooper's, the net-mender's, the chandlery where Varga lodged above the store. The main harbor was a smear of yellow light to her right, the watchman's lantern swinging on its rounds, and she moved between the two darknesses with her heart loud in her ears and nothing in her hands to explain why she was abroad at this hour.

She had given away the record. Seventy-six quarters, her own hand among them, the docket with its pencilled message — all of it left for a stranger to find or ignore. What she carried now was only observation: the lamp, the boat, the woman's face caught in the flame, the quality of waiting that was not fear but duty. She did not know if that would be enough. She did not know what enough looked like anymore.

The Customs House rose out of the fog like a ship itself, its slate roof shedding mist, its harbor-side door a black rectangle in the grey stone. She had left it unbolted hours ago, or bolted it, she could not remember. Her fingers found the latch and lifted, and the door swung inward on darkness that smelled of lamp oil and old paper and the particular stillness of a building that held its breath.

The ledger room was above. She climbed the main stair, her boots loud on the wood, no longer caring who heard. Let the watchman come. Let him find her and demand an explanation. She had spent the night watching a lamp burn where no lamp should burn, and the ordinary fears of a junior clerk caught out of bed seemed thin as copied paper.

The door to the ledger room stood open. Lamplight spilled across the threshold.

Varga sat at Wren's desk, in Wren's chair, her grey coat buttoned to the throat and her heron ring catching the flame. She held a pen — Wren's pen, the one with the worn nib she kept in the spare drawer — and she was not writing. She was turning it between her fingers, slowly, the way she turned pages when she wanted a clerk to feel the weight of each one.

"Miss Adair," she said, without looking up. "I understood you had work to finish."

Wren stopped in the doorway. The room was warm, too warm, the stove in the corner banked high against the fog. The shelves of Salt Ledgers stood in their rows, nineteen years of balanced columns, and Varga sat among them like a reader at the end of a very long book.

"I finished it," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected, the same steadiness that had carried her through the cellar confrontation, through the copying, through the fog. "Or I stopped. I'm not sure which."

Varga set the pen down on the blotter, aligned with the desk edge, the way she taught clerks to set down pens when they were done. "You went to berth nine."

It was not a question. Wren felt the fact settle between them, another piece of the arrangement clicking into place. Maren, or someone like her, had sent word. The Tidewardens and the Customs House did not speak, but they watched, and they reported, and the nineteen-year balance required that certain eyes be kept on certain movements.

"I saw the lamp," Wren said.

"And the boat."

"And the woman who tends them."

Varga nodded, a small motion, almost approval. "Maren. She has kept the signal for eleven years. Before her, it was a man named Corvin. Before him, two brothers whose names I have forgotten. The arrangement is older than any keeper."

Wren stepped into the room. The doorframe was behind her now, and the stair, and the fog. She was in Varga's space, Varga's building, Varga's nineteen years of heron seals and balanced ledgers. But she was the one who had gone to berth nine and seen what burned there, and that gave her something the chair and the ring and the grey coat could not take back.

"I left the copied record with her," Wren said. "All of it. My hand, the other clerks' hands, the docket with the message. She can burn it or keep it or hand it to whoever comes in on tomorrow night's tide. I don't know what she'll do."

For the first time, something shifted in Varga's face. Not surprise — Wren did not think Varga was capable of surprise — but a tightening around the eyes, a minute adjustment of the mask that suggested the mask had been there all along.

"That," Varga said slowly, "was not what I expected."

"You expected me to bring it to you."

"I expected you to keep it. To hold the evidence, to weigh your options, to come to me when you had calculated the angles. That is what I would have done." Varga looked at her hands, the heron ring, the fingers that had placed seventy-six seals in grey wax. "That is what I did do, nineteen years ago, when I found the first docket on my desk and the man who placed it standing in my doorway."

The harbor bell rang, closer now, counting an hour Wren had lost track of. She stood in the lamplight with empty hands and the weight of Varga's unexpected confession pressing against her chest like the roll of paper had pressed, and understood that the confrontation had shifted. This was no longer about evidence she could produce or hide. It was about witness — what she had seen, what Varga had chosen to let her see, and what either of them would do with the knowledge that the arrangement was not a secret kept by one woman alone but a structure, maintained by many, that had simply waited for someone to ask why.

"Who was he?" Wren asked. "The man in your doorway."

Varga was quiet for a long moment. The pen sat on the blotter, aligned and still. The stove ticked in the corner, cooling.

"Sit down, Miss Adair," she said. "The tide will turn twice more before morning, and I find I am tired of standing."
