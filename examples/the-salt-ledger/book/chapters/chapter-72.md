## Beat

Maren reveals that her uncle Emil, a Tidewarden pilot who died guiding the Margery Lane, passed the lamp-tending duty to her through her mother three days after the sinking; Wren learns the nineteen-year arrangement began in inherited grief rather than conspiracy, and must decide whether to interrogate the living messenger, share her own parallel loss, or physically enter the boat Emil left behind.

## Choices

- **Ask Maren about the messenger** (a new beat): interrogate — Maren yields the name of whoever brought the lamp to her mother, exposing the living hand that turned grief into system—but the asking costs their fragile trust
- **Tell Maren about the plaque** (a new beat): connect — Wren shares her own memory of the plaque and her father's lesson, building solidarity through mutual loss and maxing her confrontation with the Margery Lane's meaning in her life

## Chapter Text
Wren asked the question that the seventy-six quarters could not answer.

"Who first told you to light the lamp?"

Maren's scarred hands went still above the row of copied sheets. The fog moved through the space between them, carrying the smell of the fish market and the deeper salt of the open harbor. The lamp burned at her feet, steady and patient, and for a moment Wren thought the woman would not answer—that the question was a door Maren had learned to walk past without opening.

Then Maren sat down on the quay stone, not kneeling but settling fully, her oilskins creaking, her legs folding beneath her like someone who had spent years learning to rest in small moments between tides. She looked at the lamp, not at Wren.

"Emil," she said. "My mother's brother. He was a Tidewarden, a pilot. The kind they send out to bring ships through when the masters are impatient and the Teeth are hungry."

Wren waited. The name meant nothing to her yet, but Maren's voice had changed, the rough salt edge softened by something older, kept polished by long handling.

"He didn't sail on the Margery Lane," Maren continued. "He piloted her out. The master—Cask—wanted to catch the tide before the squall. Emil told him no. Emil always told them no, and sometimes they listened. That night, Cask didn't listen. Or maybe the squall came faster than anyone knew. Eleven men on the plaque, and Emil was the twelfth, the one they didn't carve because he wasn't ship's company, only a Tidewarden doing his work."

The harbor bell rang once, distant. Maren did not seem to hear it.

"Three days later," she said, "someone came to my mother with the lamp and the instructions. I was seventeen. I kept boats. Emil had spoken my name before he went out, told them I knew the knot that slips in a hurry, that I could tend a boat in weather. My mother gave me the lamp and the words and the time I should light it, and she never told me who brought them. Only that Emil had kept something, and that it must keep burning, and that the sea does not forgive an interrupted signal."

Wren looked at the flame between them, the small honest thing that had outlasted clerks and assessors and perhaps even the ship it summoned. She thought of her father standing beside the plaque, his hand on her shoulder, telling her what the sea did to people who were in a hurry. She thought of Varga in the cellar, her voice very quiet: *Nineteen years, Wren. Do you know how many clerks have sat at that desk in nineteen years?*

"Did you ever ask," Wren said, "what the signal was for?"

Maren's pale eyes found hers. "Every night for the first year. Every time I filled the lamp and checked the wick and set it burning in fog that swallowed everything. I asked who was coming. I asked what they carried. I asked why Emil had died for a light that no official eye ever checked." She paused. Her scarred fingers found the shape of the knot at her belt, the one that slipped in a hurry. "Then I stopped asking. The asking didn't change the tending. The lamp burned or it didn't, and the tide came in regardless, and someone on the other side still believed the signal meant safe passage. That was enough. That became the promise."

"And the payments?" Wren said. "The heron seal, the harbor account, the dockets that balance a dead ship's name?"

"I knew the lamp." Maren's voice had returned to its low register, but something trembled beneath it now, a fault line in nineteen years of patience. "The lamp and the boat and the tide. The rest was not mine to keep."

But her hands betrayed her. They moved at her sides, opening and closing, the scarred fingers finding and refinding the shape of the knot. Wren watched those hands and understood that Maren had not stopped asking—that she had only learned to ask in silence, in the trimming of wicks, in the small gestures of a vigil that outlasted the ordinary shapes of grief.

The fog shifted. The boat rocked at its mooring, the short knock of hull against fender that seemed louder now in the silence after Maren's voice. The woman sat on the quay stone with her hands open in her lap, the knot she had been tracing gone slack, all nineteen years of patience laid bare between them. Wren looked at the lamp, at the boat, at the row of copied sheets weighted with harbor pebbles, and felt the weight of what she had come to do settle against the weight of what Maren had kept burning. She had asked and been answered. The next question would not be asked in the same voice. The next question would cost something that could not be copied onto paper.
