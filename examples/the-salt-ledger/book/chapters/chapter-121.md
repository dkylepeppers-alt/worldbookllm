## Chapter Text
Wren leaned forward in her chair. The desk edge pressed a line into her ribs, familiar pressure, the same pressure she had felt for three years of copying dockets and balancing columns. But the question she asked now was not a clerk's question. It was a question about endings.

"Tell me how it ends."

Varga's hands stilled on the blotter. The heron ring faced upward, dull in the fading lamplight, and for a moment Wren thought she would deflect—would speak of tides, of ledgers, of the arrangement's own momentum as if it were a ship that steered itself.

"The lamp goes unlit," Wren said, when Varga did not answer. "The ship comes to berth nine and finds no signal. What happens then? Does Cask turn back? Does he come ashore anyway? Does someone send for the watchman, the harbor master, the Tidewarden council? Who comes looking, and what do they find?"

Varga's jaw tightened. The controlled mask she had worn for nineteen years seemed to crack along a fault line Wren could almost trace: the place where the Master Assessor ended and the woman began.

"I don't know," Varga said.

The words were small. They landed between them like a dropped pen, like a figure entered wrong in a column that had balanced for seventy-six quarters.

"You don't know," Wren repeated.

"I have never let the lamp go unlit." Varga's voice was barely above a whisper, stripped of its usual measured weight. "I have pressed the seal every quarter. I have balanced the account. I have told myself that the arrangement was the arrangement, that it existed outside my will, that I was merely the clerk of it. But I have never—not once—refused to press the seal. I have never failed to prepare the docket. I have never let the quarter pass without making the Margery Lane real on the page."

She stopped. Her hands closed into fists on the blotter, then opened again, the fingers spreading flat as if pressing something invisible.

"Cask comes when the lamp is lit," Varga continued. "He has come for seventy-six quarters. If the lamp were dark—" She paused, searching. "If the lamp were dark, he would not come. Or he would come and find no welcome, and the cargo would not move, and the payment would not be made, and the harbor account—"

"Would not balance," Wren finished.

"Would not balance." Varga's eyes met hers. "For the first time in nineteen years, the harbor account would show a gap. A ship that existed on the page but not on the water. A tithe unpaid. A column that did not add up."

The stove ticked once, twice, cooling into ash. The fog pressed against the window, erasing the harbor, the quay, the lamp at berth nine that burned for a ship that was not coming in any ordinary way. Inside the ledger room, the two of them sat with the weight of what Varga had never allowed herself to imagine: the simple act of not doing, and the cascade of consequences that would follow.

"And the Tidewardens?" Wren asked. "Maren, and Corvin before her, and the brothers whose names you forgot. What happens to them if the lamp goes dark?"

Varga's mouth tightened. "They are complicit. They have always been complicit. The lamp is their signal, their agreement, their share of the silence. If it goes dark—" She stopped again, and Wren saw something new in her face: not guilt, not relief, but fear. The fear of a woman who had spent nineteen years inside a structure she had never learned to see from outside.

"They would be exposed," Varga said slowly, as if discovering the fact as she spoke it. "Not by me. By the gap in the account. By the harbor master asking why berth nine was reserved for a ship that never came. By the watchman noticing that a lamp burned there for nineteen years and now did not."

Wren watched her work it through, the clerk's mind applying itself at last to the problem it had spent nineteen years avoiding. Varga had been so good at balance, so precise in her complicity, that she had never mapped what imbalance would look like. Never traced the lines of consequence that radiated outward from a single unlit lamp.

"And Cask?" Wren asked.

Varga was quiet for a long moment. The harbor bell rang, distant and slow, counting an hour that belonged to neither night nor morning.

"I don't know if he still lives," Varga said finally. "I have not seen him in eleven years. The dockets come through channels I do not control. The money appears. The signature is his. But whether the man who stood on my threshold with seawater in his hair is still breathing—" She spread her hands on the blotter, palms down, the heron ring facing Wren like a seal waiting for wax. "I don't know. I have not wanted to know."

The confession was complete now, or as complete as any confession ever was. Wren sat in her chair and felt the weight of what she had extracted: not a plan, not a solution, but the admission that nineteen years of control had been nineteen years of blindness, that the woman who had taught her to look carefully had spent two decades refusing to look at what she herself had built.

The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the tide turned in the cellar, pressing against foundations that had grown soft with years of brine. Wren looked at her own hands—small, ink-stained, the hands of a clerk who had copied the Margery Lane entry without knowing what it meant—and understood that the next choice would define what happened when the fog lifted.

She could demand that Varga name every person who would fall if the lamp went dark, forcing her mentor to confront the full human cost of the balance she had kept. She could ask whether Varga had ever truly tried to stop it, testing whether this confession was genuine reckoning or another polished story. Or she could simply stand and leave, forcing the confrontation out of this room and into the harbor where the lamp still burned and the fog still moved, making Varga choose whether to follow or let her walk into the dark alone.
