## Chapter Text

The Customs House of Harrowmere kept its books the way the sea kept its dead: patiently, and with no intention of giving anything back.

Wren Adair had been a junior clerk there for three years, long enough to know that the year-end reconciliation was not work anyone wanted. Every ship that came into the harbor paid the salt tithe, a duty weighed against its cargo and recorded in the Salt Ledger under the seal of the assessor who had cleared it. At the turn of the year, someone had to walk the ledger back through every quarter, page by page, and make sure that what the Customs House had been paid matched what it had written down. This year, as in the two before it, that someone was Wren.

She did not mind as much as the others thought she should. The ledger room was on the top floor, under the slates, and in the evenings the only sound was the harbor bell counting the tide. She liked the columns. She liked that a column either added up or it did not, and that when it did not, there was a reason, and the reason was written down somewhere if you were willing to look.

By the third night she had reached the merchant hulls registered before the war. Most of them were long gone, broken up for timber or sold south, their entries ending in the same faded line: _Struck from the register._ She turned the pages quickly, running her finger down the margin, until the finger stopped on its own.

_Margery Lane. Brigantine, two masts. Master: O. Cask. Tithe assessed and paid in full._

The date beside it was the first day of the autumn quarter. This autumn. Seven weeks ago.

Wren knew the _Margery Lane_. Everyone in Harrowmere did. Nineteen years ago she had gone out past the Teeth in a squall with eleven men aboard and a hold full of copper, and the Teeth had kept her. There was a plaque about it on the harbor wall. Wren's father had taken her to see it when she was small, and had told her that this was what the sea did to people who were in a hurry.

She turned back a page, then another. The _Margery Lane_ had paid its tithe in the summer quarter. And the spring. And the winter before that. She kept turning, faster now, the paper cold and slightly damp under her hands, until the ledger gave out and she had to fetch the previous volume from the shelf, and then the one before that. The entries went back without a break. Every quarter for nineteen years, a ship at the bottom of the sea had paid its taxes on time.

Every entry carried the same seal: a pressed circle of grey wax, and inside it a heron standing on one leg.

Wren sat back. The chair creaked. Below her, the bell rang the turn of the tide, and the sound went on longer than it should have in the empty room.

She knew the heron. It was on the ring Master Assessor Ilse Varga wore on her right hand, the hand she used to point out mistakes.

# break

Ilse Varga had cleared more cargo than anyone in the Customs House, and she had never, in Wren's hearing, raised her voice. She did not need to. She had a way of reading a column of figures aloud, slowly, that made the clerk who had written it wish they had chosen another trade. When Wren had first come to the Customs House, sixteen and trying not to look it, it was Varga who had taken her ledger, read it through in silence, and handed it back with a single correction in the margin and the words: _You see what others skim. Don't stop._

Wren had kept that page. It was in the drawer of her desk downstairs, under the spare nibs.

She made herself go slowly. A seal could be copied. A clerk could have used the wrong ring, or pressed an old impression into new wax, or simply carried a line forward from one quarter to the next without thinking, the way tired people did. She checked the hand. The entries were not all in one hand; they were in the hand of whichever clerk had been on the tithe desk that quarter, including, two years ago, her own.

Wren stared at that one for a long time. She did not remember writing it. She remembered the quarter, the fog that had sat on the harbor for a week, the crates of salt cod stacked to the rafters of the weighing shed. She did not remember the _Margery Lane_. But it was her hand, her slanted sevens, her habit of crossing the double-l in _full_ as if it were a t. Someone had brought her a docket with the heron seal already on it, and she had copied it into the ledger along with forty others, and thought nothing of it.

The money was real. She checked that too. Every payment had gone into the harbor account, and the harbor account balanced, as it always did. Whatever the _Margery Lane_ was, it was not a theft from the Customs House. If anything, the Customs House had been paid for something that did not exist.

Or for something that did, under a name that no one would ever look at twice.

She copied the entries onto a loose sheet, the dates and amounts and the names of the clerks, her own included, and folded the sheet into her coat. Then she put the volumes back on the shelf in the order she had found them, and stood for a while with her hand on their spines, listening to the harbor.

There was one more thing to check, and it was not in the ledger room.

Every tithe began with a docket: a slip written on the quay by the weigher who had measured the cargo, signed by the ship's master, and brought up to the tithe desk to be copied and sealed. The dockets were kept in the cellars, in boxes by year, and nobody had looked at them since they were filed. Wren took a lamp and went down.

The cellar smelled of brine and old paper. The boxes went back further than the ledgers did, and some had swollen and split where the high tides came in under the foundations. She found the current year's box easily enough and began to go through the autumn quarter.

The _Margery Lane_'s docket was near the bottom. It was on the same cheap grey paper as all the others, written in a weigher's careful capitals. _Copper ingot, forty hundredweight. Berth nine._ The master's signature was a single looping _Cask_. The heron seal sat in the corner, crisp and fresh.

Underneath, in a different hand, someone had written a line in pencil that did not belong on a tithe docket at all:

_Tell O. the lamp is lit._

Wren read it three times. Berth nine was at the far end of the north quay, past the fish market, where the Tidewardens kept their boats. The Tidewardens were the harbor pilots, the men and women who brought ships in past the Teeth, and they were older than the Customs House and did not much care for it. Nobody brought a ship into Harrowmere without a Tideward at the wheel. Nobody, as far as Wren knew, had ever brought one in to berth nine.

She was reaching for the previous year's box when she heard the cellar door open at the top of the stairs.

# break

She knew the step before she saw the lamp. Unhurried, even, a little heavier on the left foot.

"Miss Adair," said Ilse Varga. "You're working late."

Wren turned. The Master Assessor stood on the bottom stair with her lamp held low, so that it lit the floor between them and not much else. She wore her grey coat buttoned to the throat, as she always did, and the heron ring caught the light on her right hand.

"The reconciliation," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "I'm behind."

"You're never behind." Varga came down the last step. Her eyes went to the open box, and then to the docket in Wren's hand, and stayed there. For a moment the cellar was quiet enough that Wren could hear the water moving under the floor.

"I wondered who it would be," Varga said at last. "I thought, once, that it might be you."

"Then you know what I found."

"I know what you think you found." Varga set her lamp down on a crate. In its light she looked tired, and older than she had looked upstairs, and not at all surprised. "Nineteen years, Wren. Do you know how many clerks have sat at that desk in nineteen years? Every one of them copied that line. Not one of them asked."

"Who is O.?"

Varga did not answer. She looked at the pencilled line on the docket for a long moment, and when she spoke again her voice was very quiet.

"There is a ship coming in on tomorrow night's tide," she said. "It will berth where it always berths, and it will pay what it always pays, and in the morning the ledger will balance. That is all that happens, if you let it. You may think about what happens if you don't."

She picked up her lamp and turned back to the stairs. On the first step she paused, without looking round.

"Put the box back where you found it. The tide comes in under that wall."

Then she was gone, and Wren stood alone in the cellar with the docket in her hand, the water whispering under the floor, and the harbor bell beginning, far above her, to count the turn of the tide.
