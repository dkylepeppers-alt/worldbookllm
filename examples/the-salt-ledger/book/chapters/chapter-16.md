## Chapter Text
Wren set the roll on the desk and sat down. The ledger lay open before her, the page where her own slanted sevens marched across the line for the _Margery Lane_, autumn quarter, two years past. She uncapped her pen and set it beside the blotter, the posture of a clerk who had never stopped working. The lamp burned low. The harbor bell counted the tide. She did not touch the roll. She did not look at the door.

The lock turned.

It was not the watchman's key — she knew the sound of his ring, the particular scrape of iron he made — but another key, one that moved more carefully, as if the hand that held it had all the time in the world. The door opened, and the lamplight from the corridor fell across the desk and the open ledger and the cylinder of paper tied with blotter cord.

Ilse Varga stood in the doorway. She wore her grey coat buttoned to the throat, and her hair was down, which Wren had never seen, a dark line across her shoulders. She looked at the desk, and then at Wren, and the silence between them was longer than any Wren had known in the Customs House.

"You're still here," Varga said.

"I'm behind."

"You're never behind." Varga came into the room and closed the door behind her, the click of the latch loud in the stillness. She did not sit. She stood on the far side of the desk and looked down at the open page, at Wren's name in Wren's hand, at the roll of copied sheets with its ends fraying where the cord had bitten. "You sat down to be found."

Wren said nothing.

"Not by me." Varga's voice was quiet, the same quiet she used when she read a column of figures aloud and made the clerk wish for another trade. "By the watchman. By someone who would have to report it. You wanted a witness."

"I wanted it to be real."

"It was real before tonight." Varga reached out and touched the roll with one finger, the heron ring catching the lamplight. "Nineteen years, and you are the first who tried to make someone else see. The others copied the line and went home. Some of them noticed. None of them stayed."

"You told me not to stop."

Varga's hand withdrew. She looked at Wren with an expression Wren could not read — something tired, something almost fond, something closed like a door she had learned to keep latched. "I wondered," she said, "whether that was a mistake."

Below them, the watchman's bell rang the hour. Varga did not move at the sound. She stood with her back to the door and the harbor fog pressing against the window glass, and Wren understood that she was not leaving, that she had come back to the Customs House in the middle of the night because she had known, or guessed, what Wren would choose.

"The watchman will make his round again in twenty minutes," Varga said. "He will find this door unlocked, and a junior clerk asleep at her desk, and nothing out of place. Or he will find you sitting here with seventy-six quarters of copied evidence and a docket that does not belong in your hand, and he will have to tell someone. The harbormaster. The magistrate. Me."

She paused. The lamplight flickered, a draft from the window or the corridor or the slow breathing of the building itself.

"I cannot unfind you, Wren. I can only decide what happens next."

Wren looked at the roll on the desk, at her own name in the ledger, at the woman who had told her she saw what others skim. The harbor bell was still counting, measuring something that no longer felt like time.
