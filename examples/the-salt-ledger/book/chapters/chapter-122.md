## Beat

Wren accepts the heron ring from Varga, choosing to inherit the nineteen-year arrangement; the story ends with her alone at the tithe desk, the next quarter's docket before her, understanding the cost of the balance she has chosen to maintain.

## Chapter Text
Wren reached across the desk.

Her hand moved slowly, the way it moved when she copied a docket she did not fully understand—deliberate, careful, each motion chosen. She did not touch Varga's fingers. She reached past them, toward the heron ring that had pressed seventy-six seals into grey wax, and she stopped with her own hand palm-up beneath it, an open page waiting for an entry.

"Give me the ring," she said.

Varga did not move. The lamplight showed her face as it had shown it for nineteen years: controlled, deliberate, the mask of a woman who had learned that expressions were liabilities. But something flickered—not in her eyes, which remained steady, but in the set of her jaw, the angle of her head, the minute recalculation of a clerk who had just been asked to balance an account she had never prepared.

"You want to become me," Varga said. It was not a question, and it was not an accusation. It was an assessment, the way she might assess a cargo manifest: stating the contents to see if the shipmaster would correct her.

"I want to understand what the balance keeps alive." Wren kept her hand where it was, open, empty, waiting. "You said the arrangement stops only when someone stops copying the line. I have stopped. Now I am asking what happens if I start again—with my own hand, my own seal, my own justification."

Varga looked down at the ring. The heron stood on one leg, frozen in silver, the same posture it had held since her mother left it to her. She turned her hand slowly, and the heron turned with it, and the lamplight moved across its surface like tide across a stone.

"You are nineteen," Varga said. "I was twenty-four when Cask stood on my mat. I thought I was old enough to know what I was buying. I was not." She looked up, and her eyes held something Wren had not seen in three years of mentorship—not pride, not disappointment, but a kind of exhausted recognition, the look of a woman who had finally found the question she had been waiting for and discovered she did not want to answer it. "You believe you would do it differently. Better. More honestly."

"I believe I would know what I was doing when I did it."

"That," Varga said, "is what I believed."

The stove ticked in the corner, cooling. The fog pressed against the window, erasing the harbor, the quay, the lamp at berth nine that burned for a ship that was not coming in any ordinary way. Inside the ledger room, the two of them sat with Wren's open hand between them like a docket that had not yet been signed.

Varga's fingers closed into a fist. The heron disappeared into her palm, hidden, and Wren felt her own hand tense, ready to withdraw, to pull back into the fog and the night and the person she had been three days ago when the columns still added up because she had not thought to look beneath them.

But Varga did not keep the ring clenched. She opened her hand again, slowly, and this time the motion was different—not defensive, not claiming, but something older and more final. The gesture of a woman who had held something too long and had finally found the strength to set it down.

"The ring," Varga said, "was my mother's. She was a clerk, like you. She taught me to see what others skim, and she died before I learned that seeing was not the same as speaking." She looked at the heron, one leg raised, frozen in its posture of balance. "I press the seal because she pressed it. Because the heron stands on one leg, and that posture means balance, and balance meant—" She stopped. Her voice had grown thin, the controlled measure fraying at its edges. "Balance meant not having to choose. Not having to want anything beyond the next quarter, the next seal, the next column that added up because the money was real even when the ship was not."

Wren waited. Her palm remained open on the desk, an empty page.

"I have wanted to stop," Varga said, and the admission came out barely louder than the stove's ticking. "I have wanted to stop for seventy quarters, for eighty, for more than I can count without opening the ledgers. But stopping would have meant admitting that the balance was not the same as justice, and I was not strong enough to survive that admission." She looked up, and her eyes were wet, though her voice held steady. "You are strong enough. I do not know if that makes you better suited or merely more dangerous."

She turned her hand over. The heron ring lay in her palm, silver against skin that had grown pale from years of lamplight and grey wax. She did not drop it. She placed it, carefully, deliberately, the way she placed a seal on a docket that needed her approval to become real.

The ring was cold when it touched Wren's skin. Heavier than she had expected, or perhaps that was only the weight of what it had pressed into wax seventy-six times. She closed her fingers around it, and the heron's form pressed into her palm, one leg raised, frozen in its posture of perpetual balance.

"The seventy-seventh quarter begins in three days," Varga said. "The docket will arrive as it always arrives. The weigher will write his careful capitals. The ship will pay its salt tithe. You will copy the entry, you will press the seal, you will balance the column." She stood, slowly, her hand empty now, her fingers curling and uncurling as if searching for a weight they had carried too long to remember how to release. "Or you will not. That choice will be yours, as it was never mine, because you will know what you are doing when you do it, and I—" She stopped at the doorframe, her grey coat catching the lamplight, her silhouette thin and suddenly old against the darkness of the stair. "I did not know. I did not let myself know. That was the price I paid, and you have already paid a different one by asking questions I spent nineteen years learning not to ask."

The door closed behind her with a sound like a ledger falling shut.

Wren sat alone in the ledger room, the heron ring in her hand, the lamplight holding her in its small circle while the fog pressed against the glass and the harbor bell counted an hour that belonged to neither night nor morning. She turned the ring slowly, watching the heron catch the flame and lose it, catch and lose, a small silver tide.

Then she slipped it onto her right hand.

It fit poorly, loose at the knuckle, the metal warmed by her palm but still carrying the shape of Varga's finger. Wren spread her hand on the blotter and looked at it—the heron standing on one leg, the posture of balance, the seal that would press grey wax into the next docket and the next and the next, as long as she chose to press it.

She reached for the pen. The nib was dry, the inkwell low, but there was enough. She pulled a fresh sheet from the drawer, the paper crisp and unmarked, and she wrote at the top in her own careful hand: *Margery Lane, seventy-seventh quarter, salt tithe due.*

The columns awaited their figures. The seal awaited its wax. The harbor bell rang again, distant and slow, and Wren Adair, nineteen years old, sat at the tithe desk with the heron ring on her hand and the next quarter's docket before her, understanding at last what it meant to see what others skimmed—and to keep seeing, and to keep copying, and to let the balance hold.
