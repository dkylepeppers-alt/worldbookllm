## Chapter Text
The exact figure," Wren said. "The first docket. What was the amount?"

Varga's hands stilled on the blotter. The heron ring faced upward, catching the flame, and for a moment the only motion in the room was the stove's cooling tick.

"Triple tithe," Varga said. "I told you. He offered—"

"You said he offered double, then triple." Wren leaned forward, her voice quiet but precise, the tone she used when a column refused to balance. "Which was it on the first docket? The one you pressed yourself, in your own hand. Double or triple?"

Varga's mouth tightened. She looked at her hands, the ring, the pen aligned with the desk edge. "Triple," she said, but the word came out slower than her usual measured cadence. "It must have been triple. He said double, then triple, and I would not have agreed for less than—"

"Than what?" Wren asked. "Than twelve pounds? Than fifteen? What was the standard rate that year, and what did the weigher write on the docket, and what sum did you copy into the ledger?"

Varga was silent. The lamplight showed her face clearly now—the grey beneath the eyes, the slight parting of lips that had held nineteen years of confession without ever once producing the actual arithmetic.

"I don't remember," Varga said. The words were small, almost swallowed by the room's held breath. "I remember pressing the seal. I remember his hair, still wet. I remember the smell of brine in my doorway. I do not remember the figure."

Wren felt the answer land. It had the quality of truth—not the polished truth of the story, but the raw truth underneath. Varga had performed her guilt for nineteen years, reciting a script that sounded like confession, but she had never checked the docket against her memory. She had never needed to. No one had asked.

"You told me double, then triple," Wren said. "But you don't know which was first. You don't know if the amount changed later, or when, or why. You have been telling yourself a story about a bribe, and the story has replaced the transaction."

Varga's jaw tightened. For a moment the controlled mask held, then something cracked—the subtle collapse of a structure that had borne weight too long. "What does the figure matter?" she asked, but her voice had thinned, the authority fraying. "The principle matters. The choice matters. I agreed to let a dead ship pay taxes so that a living man could hide behind it."

"The figure matters," Wren said, "because you have been counting the dead in quarters for nineteen years, and you don't even know what they were worth. You don't know if the price went up or down. You don't know if Cask ever paid late, or short, or not at all. You have a ledger in the cellar that remembers everything you have forgotten, and tomorrow night's ship will come to berth nine whether you know the terms of your own bargain or not."

The fog moved against the window, erasing the harbor. Varga sat in Wren's chair at Wren's desk, surrounded by nineteen years of Salt Ledgers that balanced because someone had decided long ago that balance was worth more than honesty, and for the first time she looked like what she was: a woman who had made a choice in a single night and had spent nineteen years avoiding the details.

The stove ticked once, twice. Somewhere below, the tide turned in the cellar, pressing against foundations that had grown soft with years of brine. Wren looked at her own hands—small, ink-stained, the hands of a clerk who trusted columns because columns either added up or they did not—and understood that the next step would define what happened when the fog lifted.

She could go down to the cellar alone, find the first docket, verify the figure that Varga had forgotten and break the intimacy of this room with the physical fact of ink on paper. She could offer her own hands to the search, turn her clerk's skill toward helping Varga confront the record she had avoided for nineteen years, and transform this interrogation into something they did together. Or she could stand and leave the story unfinished, walk out into the fog with knowledge but no proof, and force Varga to wonder what she would do when the harbor bell rang for tomorrow night's tide.

Varga waited. The lamplight held them both in its small circle, and beyond it the fog moved, patient and indifferent, carrying the smell of the sea and the weight of a story that no longer added up.
