## Beat

Wren returns to the Customs House without the copied record and finds Varga waiting in the ledger room; the confrontation shifts from evidence to witness, as Wren has nothing physical to bargain with but carries the direct knowledge of berth nine, and Varga seems unexpectedly relieved rather than threatened by the loss of the evidence.

## Choices

- **Take the empty chair** (a new beat): acceptance — Wren accepts the offered seat and enters Varga's confessional space, trading the moral high ground of standing for the knowledge Varga seems ready to share, but the chair puts her physically below her mentor's eye level
- **Ask about the eleven men first** (a new beat): confrontation — Wren remains standing and asks Varga to account for the eleven men who did not survive the Margery Lane, refusing the personal narrative until the moral ledger is addressed; the question changes what Varga can offer from explanation to justification
- **Go down to the cellar boxes** (a new beat): verification — Wren walks out of the ledger room and down to the cellar where the docket boxes wait, choosing to verify what Varga claims against the physical records rather than accept any confession at face value; she trades immediate answers for independent confirmation

## Chapter Text
She climbed the stair. The fog had followed her in through the harbor door, clinging to her coat and finding the gaps between her collar and her neck, a cold finger against skin that had grown numb from hours on the quay. Her boots found the worn centers of the steps automatically, the path she had walked a thousand times, but each landing seemed farther than she remembered, as if the building had stretched in her absence.

The ledger room door stood open. Lamplight spilled across the threshold in a yellow line that cut the dark corridor in two.

Varga sat at Wren's desk, in Wren's chair, her grey coat buttoned to the throat. She held a pen—Wren's pen, the one with the worn nib—and she was not writing. She turned it between her fingers, slowly, the way she turned pages when she wanted a clerk to feel the weight of each one. The heron ring caught the flame and held it, a small steady eye in the room's dimness.

"Miss Adair." Varga did not look up. "I understood you had work to finish."

Wren stopped in the doorway. The room was warm, too warm, the stove in the corner banked high against the fog. The shelves of Salt Ledgers stood in their rows, nineteen years of balanced columns, and Varga sat among them like a reader at the end of a very long book. But something was different from the other times Wren had found her waiting. The pen in her hands moved without purpose. The shoulders beneath the grey coat held a stiffness that was not her usual controlled posture.

"I finished it," Wren said. Her voice came out steady, the same steadiness that had carried her through the cellar, through the copying, through the fog to berth nine and back. "Or I stopped. I'm not sure which."

Varga set the pen down on the blotter, aligned it with the desk edge. "You went to berth nine."

It was not a question. Wren felt the fact settle between them, another piece of the arrangement clicking into place. Maren, or the fog, or simply the harbor's network of watching eyes, had sent word ahead of her.

"I saw the lamp," Wren said.

"And the boat."

"And the keeper."

Varga nodded, a small motion. "Maren. She has kept the signal for eleven years. Before her, Corvin. Before him, two brothers whose names even I have forgotten." She looked up then, and her eyes were tired—more tired than Wren had ever seen them, the skin beneath them grey with sleeplessness that no amount of lamplight could warm. "The arrangement is older than any keeper. You know that now. You have seen it living."

Wren stepped into the room. The doorframe was behind her, and the stair, and the fog. She was in Varga's space, Varga's building, Varga's nineteen years of heron seals and balanced ledgers. But she was empty-handed. The roll of paper that had pressed against her ribs for hours was gone, left on the quay stones for Maren to find or burn or carry to whatever council the Tidewardens kept. What Wren had now was only witness: the lamp, the boat, Maren's scarred hands gathering the sheets, the quality of waiting that was not fear but something older and more patient.

"I left the record with her," Wren said. "All of it. My hand, the other clerks' hands, the docket with the message. She can do what she wants with it."

For the first time, something shifted in Varga's face. Not surprise—Wren did not think Varga was capable of surprise—but a loosening, a minute adjustment of some inner tension that suggested she had been bracing for something harder than this admission.

"That," Varga said slowly, "was not what I expected."

"You expected me to bring it to you."

"I expected you to keep it." Varga looked at her hands, the heron ring, the fingers that had placed seventy-six seals in grey wax. "To hold the evidence, to weigh your options, to come to me when you had calculated the angles. That is what I would have done. That is what I did do, nineteen years ago, when I found the first docket on my desk and the man who placed it standing in my doorway."

The harbor bell rang, closer now, counting an hour Wren had lost track of. She stood in the lamplight with empty hands and the weight of Varga's unexpected confession pressing against her chest, and understood that the confrontation had shifted. This was no longer about evidence she could produce or hide. It was about witness—what she had seen, what Varga had chosen to let her see, and what either of them would do with the knowledge that the arrangement was not a secret kept by one woman alone but a structure, maintained by many, that had simply waited for someone to ask why.

"Who was he?" Wren asked. "The man in your doorway."

Varga was quiet. The pen sat on the blotter, aligned and still. The stove ticked in the corner, cooling. Outside, the fog pressed against the window glass, a grey weight that made the room feel smaller than it was, a cell rather than a workplace, the two of them sealed inside with nineteen years of questions that had finally found voice.

"Sit down, Miss Adair," Varga said. "The tide will turn twice more before morning, and I find I am tired of standing."
