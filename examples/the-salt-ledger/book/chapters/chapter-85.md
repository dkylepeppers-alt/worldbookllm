## Beat

Wren reaches her lodging and tries to rest, but the knowledge she carries keeps her awake; the morning comes with no plan and no protection.

## Choices

- **Go to the Customs House and wait at her desk** (a new beat): confrontation — Wren returns to the Customs House before anyone else and sits at the tithe desk, forcing Varga to find her there and account for what she knows, but risking that Varga controls the meeting entirely.
- **Find the plaque on the harbor wall** (a new beat): mourning — Wren walks to the harbor wall to stand before the Margery Lane plaque, confronting her childhood memory and the reality of eleven dead men whose ship still pays taxes, which may unmoor her from the urgency of acting.
- **Return to berth nine and break the signal** (a new beat): sabotage — Wren goes to the Tidewardens at berth nine and tells Maren she will help keep the lamp unlit tonight, actively breaking the arrangement and forcing the ship to show itself or turn back.

## Chapter Text
Wren walked to her lodging.

The narrow street was empty, the fog thinning to a grey veil that clung to the upper windows. She climbed the stairs to the room she had rented for two years, the key finding the lock by habit rather than attention. Inside, the air was cold and still, the small stove unlit, the bed made from the morning before when she had left for the reconciliation without knowing what she would find.

She sat on the edge of the bed and pulled off her boots. The damp had soaked through to her stockings, and her toes were white and numb. She did not light the lamp. The grey window was enough, and darkness felt safer than illumination. She lay back with her coat still on, the empty space inside it where the roll had been pressing against her ribs like a missing tooth.

Sleep did not come.

She closed her eyes and saw the lamp at berth nine, steady and patient, burning where no lamp should need to burn. She saw Maren's scarred hands hovering over the row of sheets, the wetness on her cheeks, the way she had turned toward the water and said, *What do you want me to do with seventy-six quarters of a light I thought was keeping people safe?* She saw her own sevens, slanted and careful, copied into the Salt Ledger without knowing what they served. She saw Varga in the cellar, her grey coat buttoned to the throat, her heron ring catching the lamplight as she pointed out the way the columns balanced.

The harbor bell rang, distant and slow. Wren opened her eyes. The window had grown lighter without her noticing, the grey shifting from the color of fog to the color of dawn. She had not slept. Her body ached with stillness, her mind turning the same questions like a mill wheel that would not stop for rest.

She rose. The stove was cold, and she had no appetite. She washed her face in the basin, the water so cold it made her gasp, and looked at herself in the small glass above the washstand. A young woman with dark circles under her eyes, wearing a clerk's coat that had been damp twice in one night. She looked like someone who had seen something and did not yet know what to do with the seeing.

The morning came without a plan. She had no evidence now—Maren had the sheets, the proof of seventy-six quarters, the docket with its pencilled message. She had only knowledge: the lamp, the boat, the woman's name, the ship that would come on tonight's tide. She had no protection. If Varga had already heard that Wren was seen at berth nine, the reckoning would come when Varga chose it. If Maren had spoken to the Tidewardens, they would know her too. If the watchman had noticed her irregular movements, the questions might already be forming in rooms she could not see into.

Wren buttoned her coat. The gesture felt empty without the roll of paper inside, but she performed it anyway, the familiar motion of preparing for a day's work at the Customs House. She stood by the window and watched the street below begin to wake: a fishwife with a basket, a boy carrying kindling, the ordinary commerce of a port that had never stopped to wonder why one dead brigantine still paid its salt.

The tide would turn. The ship would come. Varga would place her seal. And Wren Adair, who had given away the only proof she possessed, would have to decide what careful attention meant when it could no longer be written down and weighted with pebbles on a quay.

The morning light strengthened, indifferent, measuring something she could no longer name.
