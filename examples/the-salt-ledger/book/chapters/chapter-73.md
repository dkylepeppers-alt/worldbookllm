## Chapter Text
Wren walked. The fog took the lamp first, then the sound of Maren's boots, then even the crab traps where she had crouched for hours. She kept one hand on the warehouse wall and let her boots find the gaps between cobbles, the way she had come, but the quay felt different now—measured not in distance but in what she no longer carried.

The emptiness was physical. She kept reaching for the cylinder of paper, her right hand moving to her coat as if to check its weight, and finding only cloth and the damp of fog. Seventy-six quarters, three of them in her own hand, now pressed against Maren's oilskins in a stack she had not even rolled. She had come to berth nine to see, and seeing had made her give away the only thing that proved she had not imagined what she found.

The harbor bell rang, distant and directionless. She tried to count the strokes and lost track, the numbers breaking apart in her mind like wet paper. The fog smelled different here, past the fish market, carrying the thinner salt of the main harbor and the deeper rot of the mudflats where the tide had gone out. She walked through it without choosing a direction, letting her feet carry her toward the Customs House because that was where her days began, and then correcting, turning toward the narrower streets where her lodging stood, and then correcting again, because neither direction felt like arrival.

She was not free. She had thought, when she set the roll down at the edge of the lamplight, that she was laying down a burden. But the knowledge remained: the lamp, the boat, Maren's scarred hands gathering the sheets, the ship that would come on tomorrow night's tide. She had traded proof for witness, evidence for complicity. Maren knew her name now. Maren knew her sevens, slanted and careful. Whatever Maren did with the sheets—burn them, hide them, carry them to some Tidewarden council—she would do knowing that Wren Adair of the tithe desk had brought them, and had seen, and had chosen to walk away without them.

The quay widened. She could see the watchman's lantern now, a pale smear against the grey where the main harbor began, and beyond it the darker shapes of ships at mooring, the ordinary commerce of a port that had never stopped to wonder why one dead brigantine still paid its salt. She stood at the corner where the north quay met the harbor proper, the fog moving through the space between buildings, and understood that she had reached a different kind of ledger. No columns to balance, no dockets to copy. Only the account of what she would do with the night that remained.

The watchman's lantern swayed, counting some round Wren could not see. She looked left, toward the Customs House where Varga's heron ring waited in a drawer or on a hand. She looked right, toward the narrow street that led to her lodging, to the bed she had not slept in, to the morning that would come regardless of whether she rested. And she looked straight ahead, past the watchman's light, toward the harbor wall where the plaque hung with eleven names and her father's voice saying what the sea did to people who were in a hurry.

Three directions. Three accounts to settle. The tide whispered against the stone, indifferent, already turning toward tomorrow night's appointment.
