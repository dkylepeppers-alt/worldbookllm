## Chapter Text
Wren stood. The fog clung to her shoulders as she stepped out from beside the crab traps, her boots finding the gap between shadow and light. The woman in oilskins turned fully this time, no longer a silhouette against the water but a person with a broad face and greying hair pulled back under her knitted cap, her eyes the pale color of harbor water in winter.

"I am Wren Adair," she said. "Customs House. Tithe desk."

"I know what desk you sit at." The woman's voice was still low, but the rough edge had softened, or perhaps Wren was simply close enough to hear beneath it. "I know your hand, too. I've seen your sevens on the dockets. Slanted, careful. You write like someone who expects to be checked."

Wren felt the words land. Her own handwriting, read by eyes she had never imagined looking back. "You know the clerks' hands?"

"I know the ones that matter. The ones that write careful and copy true, and the ones that write sloppy and copy anyway." The woman looked down at the roll of paper still lying on the stone between them. "You're the first to bring the copying out here. The first to set it down where the lamp could read it."

"What does the lamp read?"

The woman almost smiled. She knelt and picked up the roll, not unrolling it, only weighing it in her scarred hands. "The lamp reads patience. The lamp reads that someone is still tending what the sea didn't take." She set the roll down again, closer to Wren this time, as if returning something borrowed. "My name is Maren. I keep the boats at berth nine, and I light the lamp when the message comes, and I do not ask who sends the message because the Tidewardens have always known things the Customs House doesn't need to learn."

"Nineteen years," Wren said.

"Nineteen years." Maren's pale eyes held hers. "You want to know if O. Cask is alive. You want to know if the Margery Lane is still sailing. You want to know why your mentor's seal is on every docket and why the harbor account balances when the ship that pays into it is supposed to be at the bottom of the Teeth."

"Yes."

"I don't know all of that. I know the lamp, and the boat, and the tide. I know that tomorrow night's ship will come in because the lamp is lit tonight, and because the lamp has been lit every quarter for nineteen years, and because someone on the other side of the water still believes the signal means something." Maren stepped closer, close enough that Wren could smell the salt in her oilskins, the lamp oil on her fingers. "What I don't know is what you want to do with what you find. You came here to see. Seeing is cheap. Doing is where the cost lives."

The flame between them burned steady, a small honest thing in all the fog. Wren looked at Maren's hands, at the roll of paper that contained every quarter of the lie, at the boat ready in the dark with its oars in the locks. She had stepped into the light. She could not step back into the same darkness.
