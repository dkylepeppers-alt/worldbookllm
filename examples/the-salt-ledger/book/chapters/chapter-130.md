## Beat

## Chapter Text
Wren let her hand fall to the desk edge. The open palm that had waited for the ring closed slowly, deliberately, into a fist that rested on the blotter like a seal pressed in anger rather than wax.

"You can pass it to me," she said, "or you can watch it burn."

The words hung in the lamplight, heavier than the fog pressing against the glass. Wren heard them as Varga must hear them: not the voice of the apprentice who had sat at this desk for three years copying dockets and accepting corrections, but the voice of someone who had walked to berth nine and seen the lamp, who had left evidence with Maren and returned with empty hands, who had listened to nineteen years of confession and found the bottom of the column wanting.

Varga's face did not change. The mask held—the controlled, deliberate expression that had faced clerks and shipmasters and harbor inspectors for seventy-six quarters. But something moved beneath it, a current Wren had learned to read during three years of marginal notes and slow readings aloud. Not fear. Something more personal. Something that looked, in the fading lamplight, like the end of a thing she had not known was still alive between them.

"You would destroy me," Varga said. It was not a question.

"I would expose the arrangement. You are part of it."

"The arrangement is not me. The arrangement is Maren at berth nine, and the weigher on the quay, and the watchman whose rounds you have already disrupted twice tonight. The arrangement is the harbor account that balances and the ships that come in regardless of what clerks write in ledgers." Varga's hands remained flat on the blotter, the heron ring still visible, still unyielded. "You would burn more than me, Miss Adair. You would burn the only person who ever told you your careful attention mattered."

Wren felt the words land. They had the quality of truth—the raw, unpolished truth that Varga had kept beneath her measured confessions, the figure that appeared when you carried the subtraction further than you intended. Three years of corrections in the margins. The spare nibs drawer where Wren kept Varga's first correction note. The slow, patient voice reading a column aloud, making the clerk who wrote it wish they had chosen another trade, and then the quieter voice afterward: *You see what others skim. Don't stop.*

"That was genuine," Wren said. It was not quite a question, and not quite an accusation.

"Yes." Varga's voice had dropped to the register she used in the small hours, when the ledger room emptied and the harbor bell counted tides that no one else was awake to hear. "It was genuine when I said it. It was genuine every time I said it. It is genuine now, which is why I am asking you—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened, the minute adjustment of a woman who had learned that expressions were liabilities, and who was discovering that some liabilities could not be avoided. "I am asking you not to make me choose between the arrangement and the apprentice who learned to see through it."

The stove ticked in the corner, cooling. The fog pressed against the window, erasing the harbor, the quay, the lamp at berth nine that burned for a ship that was not coming in any ordinary way. Wren sat with her fist on the blotter and understood that she had crossed a line that was not drawn in ink or wax but in the accumulated weight of three years of mentorship and nineteen years of secrets. The threat had been a lever, meant to pry the ring from Varga's hand. But levers worked in both directions, and she felt the pressure now against her own footing, the ground shifting beneath the moral clarity she had carried since the cellar.

"I am not asking you to choose," Wren said, but the words sounded hollow even to her, because choosing was exactly what she was demanding.

Varga looked at her. The grey beneath her eyes had deepened, the lines around her mouth grown sharp in the fading lamplight. She did not answer immediately. She reached slowly across the blotter—not toward Wren's fist, but toward the pen that lay aligned beside the inkwell, the same pen Wren had used for three years to copy dockets she did not understand. She lifted it, examined the nib, set it down again with the precise alignment of a clerk who could not abide a crooked line.

"You have threatened me," Varga said. "You have threatened the arrangement. You have done so from this desk, in this room, using the patience and the precision that I taught you. I cannot decide whether that is the highest use of your education or its final corruption." She looked up, and her eyes held something Wren had not seen before—not relief, not guilt, but a kind of exhausted assessment, the look of a woman who had finally found the question she had been waiting for and discovered she did not want to answer it. "But I will tell you what I told myself nineteen years ago, when Cask stood on my mat with seawater in his hair. The arrangement does not stop for conscience. It stops only when someone chooses to stop copying the line. You have stopped. What I do not yet know is whether you have the will to make others stop with you, or whether you are hoping that threatening me will relieve you of the burden of deciding."

The harbor bell rang, distant and slow. The fog pressed against the glass. And Wren sat with her fist on the blotter and understood that the next moment would define not only what happened to the Margery Lane but what remained of the three years that had brought her to this desk, this room, this woman who had taught her to see and was now watching to see what she would do with sight.
