## Chapter Text
Wren told her about the plaque.

Not the words carved into the harbor wall—she could not remember those, had never been able to recall whether the names were listed in full or only the master's—but the moment of standing there, her father's hand on her shoulder, his voice saying what the sea did to people who were in a hurry. She told Maren about the copper in the hold, the eleven men, the way her father had spoken as if the Margery Lane were a lesson rather than a tragedy. She told her about coming back to that plaque years later, walking past it on her way to the Customs House every morning, and how she had stopped reading it because the names had become background, stone and weather and the ordinary grief of a harbor town.

Maren went still.

It was not the stillness of someone listening. It was the stillness of a boat when the wind drops, when the sails hang empty and the only motion is the slow rock of water against the hull. Her scarred hands, broad and competent, hung at her sides. The lamplight caught the grey in her hair where it escaped her knitted cap.

"You never read the names," Maren said. Not a question.

"I was too small. And then I was not small, and I did not look."

Maren turned away from her, toward the water. The fog had thinned enough to show the black line of the horizon, the faint difference between sea and sky that meant the tide was still turning. "I knew three of them," she said. "Four, if you count the boy who scrubbed the deck and never took his pilot's exam. He would have been a Tidewarden by now. He would have been standing where I'm standing, keeping this lamp, if the Teeth had not kept him first."

The harbor bell rang once, distant. Maren did not seem to hear it.

"The lamp was not mine," she said. "Not at first. It was Emil's. He was my mother's brother, and he was the one who knew the signal, who knew what the lamp meant and when to light it and who would be coming in from the fog to find it burning. He was the one who taught me the knot for the painter, the one that slips in a hurry. He said every Tidewarden boat should be ready to cast off with one hand, because the sea does not wait for you to use two."

She turned back. Her pale eyes had changed, the harbor-water color darkened by something that was not the lamplight.

"He died on the Margery Lane," she said. "Not as crew—he was piloting her out, guiding her past the Teeth because the master was in a hurry and wanted to catch the tide. Eleven men, and Emil was the twelfth, the one they did not put on the plaque because he was not ship's company, only a Tidewarden doing his work. The Teeth kept them all. And three days later, someone came to my mother with the lamp and the instructions, and she gave them to me because I was the one in the family who kept boats, and because Emil had spoken my name before he went out."

Wren thought of the plaque she had never read, the names she had let become stone. She thought of her own hand, slanted sevens on dockets that kept a dead ship alive, and how she had copied those entries without knowing that one of the men who died had taught the woman before her how to tie a knot that slipped in a hurry.

"Nineteen years," Maren said, "and I have never known if the lamp was for the ship that sank or for something else that rose in its place. I only knew that Emil kept it, and that he died keeping it, and that someone had to continue what he began because the water does not forgive an interrupted signal."

The flame between them burned steady, consuming oil at a rate Wren could now measure against a lifetime. She saw Maren's patience differently—not duty, not conspiracy, but a form of mourning that had outlasted the ordinary shapes of grief. The lamp was a grave marker that burned instead of weathering. The waiting was a conversation with the dead.

"The dockets," Wren said slowly. "The payments. They kept coming, and the harbor account balanced, and the Customs House never asked. Did you know what the money was for?"

"I knew the lamp." Maren's voice had returned to its low register, the rough salt edge that came from wind and years. "The lamp and the boat and the tide. The rest was not mine to keep."

But her hands were not still now. They moved at her sides, opening and closing, the scarred fingers finding the shape of a knot that could be tied in darkness, that could be slipped with one hand while the other held an oar. Wren watched those hands and understood that Maren had asked her own questions, nineteen years ago, and had chosen to keep the lamp burning rather than demand answers that might have put it out.

The fog shifted. The boat rocked at its mooring, the short knock of hull against fender that Wren had heard when she first approached. The arrangement breathed around them, patient and living, and Wren stood in its heart with the complete record still on the stone between them and a woman who had kept a dead man's signal because the alternative was to admit that the sea had taken everything, even the right to stop waiting.

She could unroll the copied sheets and make Maren see what nineteen years of heron seals looked like laid out on quay stone. She could leave the roll where it lay and walk back into the fog, preserving what she had found here and letting Maren keep her grief undisturbed. Or she could speak the thing she had not said to anyone, not even to herself in the ledger room with the volumes open and her pen moving steadily across the page: that her own hand was among them, that she had copied the line too, and that she did not know if she was here to end the arrangement or to finally understand it.
