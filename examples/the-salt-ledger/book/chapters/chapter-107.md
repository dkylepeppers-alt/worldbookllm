## Chapter Text
Wren looked at her own hands.

They were small hands, ink-stained at the knuckles, the right middle finger carrying the faint callus that three years of pen-holding had built. She turned them palm-up on the desk edge, the way she might present a docket for inspection, and she felt the weight of what they had done without knowing.

"I copied the line too," she said.

Varga's head came up from the blotter. The heron ring caught the lamplight, steady, but her eyes had lost the remote quality they had held since the confession began.

"Three times," Wren continued. "The winter quarter before last. The spring quarter of last year. The autumn quarter just past. I wrote the Margery Lane in my own hand, I sealed the docket with the heron seal you left ready, I balanced the column and went home to sleep. I did not look at the ship's name. I did not remember the plaque my father showed me. I was careful, and thorough, and I did not ask why a ship that sank nineteen years ago still paid its salt tithe in the same amount every quarter."

She stopped. Her hands remained on the desk edge, offered like evidence, and she found that saying it aloud had changed something in the room's chemistry. The accusation she had carried since the cellar—Varga's fraud, Varga's seal, Varga's nineteen years—had been a single column, a clean line of blame. Now there were two columns. Now there was a ledger that balanced between them.

"You were doing your work," Varga said. The words were quiet, almost gentle, but Wren heard the distance in them—the same distance she had used herself, in the early hours of the reconciliation, when she had told herself that a clerk's duty was to copy accurately, not to question what was given.

"So were you," Wren said.

The reply landed. Varga's jaw tightened, not with anger but with recognition, and for a moment the nineteen years between them collapsed. Two women at a desk, two sets of ink-stained hands, two justifications that had served the same lie. Wren watched the understanding move through Varga's face—the realization that the apprentice had become a mirror, and that the mirror showed not only the mentor's crime but her own.

"Yes," Varga said. "So was I."

The stove ticked in the corner, cooling now, the embers sinking into grey ash. The fog pressed against the window, erasing the harbor, the quay, the lamp at berth nine that burned for a ship that was not coming in any ordinary way. Inside the ledger room, the two of them sat with their shared complicity spread between them like a sheet that neither had written alone.

"What do we do," Wren asked, "with a ledger that has both our hands in it?"

Varga did not answer immediately. She looked at Wren's hands, small and ink-stained on the desk edge, and then at her own, the heron ring dull in the fading lamplight. The silence stretched, not hostile, not comfortable—simply the silence of two accountants confronting a sum that neither could compute alone.

"The morning will come," Varga said finally. "The tide will turn. The ship will come to berth nine, or it will not, and the ledger will need its next entry regardless. I have spent nineteen years learning that the arrangement does not stop for conscience. It stops only when someone chooses to stop copying the line."

She looked up, and her eyes held something Wren had not seen before—not relief, not guilt, but a question, directed outward for the first time since the confession began.

"You have stopped," Varga said. "I can see that. What I do not yet know is what you intend to do with the stopping."

The harbor bell rang, distant and slow, counting an hour that belonged to neither night nor morning. Wren sat at her own desk with her hands still palm-up on the edge, and understood that the next choice would define not only what happened to the Margery Lane but what kind of clerk she would be when the fog lifted. She could demand the rest of Varga's account, the full reckoning that nineteen years of quarterly silence had never allowed. She could ask how the arrangement ended, forcing Varga to confront whether confession was merely another form of continuation. Or she could reach across the desk and ask for the heron ring itself, proposing to become what Varga had been—not to break the system but to inherit it, and see whether the mentor would pass the burden or finally refuse.
