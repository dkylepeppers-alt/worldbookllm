- **Keep the record hidden and ask what the ledger cannot record** (a new beat): probe — she keeps the evidence concealed and forces Varga to explain herself to a clerk who still holds the power to expose everything, but the secrecy also means Varga may not take the conversation seriously enough to reveal the full truth
- **Spread the copied sheets across the blotter** (a new beat): accuse — she makes the fraud tangible and visible, turning the private mentorship into a formal accounting, but the evidence on the desk becomes vulnerable to seizure, burning, or denial

## Chapter Text
Wren went back.

The fog received her like a held breath, swallowing the quay behind her until berth nine was only a memory of lamplight and the shape of a woman in oilskins standing watch. She kept her hand on the warehouse walls, granite blocks giving way to timber siding, until the Customs House rose before her with its harbor door still unbolted from her departure. She slipped through and shot the bolt behind her, the sound deadened by the fog that had followed her in.

The corridor smelled of nothing but itself: old plaster, lamp oil from the sconces the watchman had trimmed, the particular silence of a building that had emptied hours ago and would not fill again for hours more. The roll of paper inside her coat had warmed to her body temperature, a cylinder of seventy-six quarters pressing against her ribs as if it were breathing.

She did not go to the cellars. Varga had found her there once already, and the cellar held the weight of water moving under the floor, the whispered admission that O. Cask had swum when eleven men had not. Wren climbed the main stair instead, her boots finding the worn center of each step where twenty years of clerks had walked before her. The ledger room was on the top floor, under the slates, and she had left it dark and bolted.

The door was unbolted. A line of light showed beneath it.

Wren stopped on the landing. The watchman's rounds would not bring him here until the five o'clock pass; she had learned his pattern during the months of evening work Varga had assigned her, the extra ledgers, the reconciliations that no one else wanted. The light inside was steady, not the watchman's swinging lantern. Someone had lit the desk lamp and settled in to wait.

She pushed the door open.

Varga sat at Wren's own desk, the one by the window that looked out on fog where the harbor should be. She had not opened the ledger. She had not touched the spare nibs drawer where Wren kept the correction note from three years ago. She sat with her hands folded on the blotter, the heron ring catching the lamplight, and she looked up without surprise.

"You came back," Varga said.

"I still work here."

"Yes." Varga's voice was the same quiet she had used in the cellar, the same measured pace, but here in the ledger room it carried differently. Here it was the voice of assessment, of the woman who had read Wren's first ledger and found the single error that proved she was paying attention. "You could have stayed at berth nine. You could have walked home through the fog and let tomorrow be another day. You came back to the building where the records are kept. That means something."

Wren came into the room. The door swung shut behind her, and she did not bolt it. The lamplight held them both in a circle of yellow that reached the shelves of Salt Ledgers and stopped, the spines fading into darkness beyond. She could feel the roll of paper against her side, the copied names of clerks long gone, her own hand repeated across the years.

"I saw the lamp," Wren said.

"I know. Maren told me you had been there, when she came to report the wick."

Maren. The name settled into place, the harbor name Wren had almost summoned on the quay. Varga spoke it without emphasis, a fact among other facts, and Wren understood that the network of the arrangement was wider than she had allowed herself to imagine. Not only the heron seal and the dockets and the payments that balanced. Not only the lamp and the boat and the woman who tended them. Varga knew the keeper's name. Varga had been told, already, that Wren had reached berth nine and seen what burned there.

"Then you know what I saw," Wren said.

"I know what Maren saw. A clerk in a Customs House coat, crouched beside crab traps, watching a light that no clerk has ever questioned." Varga unfolded her hands. The heron ring caught the light again, the bird standing on one leg, patient and still. "She did not disturb you. She has learned patience too. Nineteen years of tending a lamp teaches you that the people who come to watch are not always enemies. Sometimes they are only the next ones who need to understand."

The fog pressed against the window, a grey weight that made the room feel smaller than it was. Wren stood with her back to the door and the roll of paper heavy inside her coat, and understood that Varga had prepared this scene as carefully as she prepared anything. The unbolted door. The lit lamp. The admission that she knew where Wren had been and what Wren had seen. These were not threats. They were the architecture of a conversation Varga had decided it was time to have.

"The ship comes in tomorrow night," Varga said. "The arrangement continues because it serves something that the ledger does not record. You have spent one night learning what the arrangement looks like from the outside. You have the complete record" — her eyes went to the bulge beneath Wren's coat, brief and certain — "and you have the face of one keeper. What you do not yet have is the reason."

She stood. The chair creaked, a sound too loud in the quiet room, and she moved to the window where the fog made a mirror of the glass. In it Wren could see her own reflection, blurred and uncertain, standing in a clerk's coat with nineteen years of evidence against her heart.

"You came back," Varga said again, to the window, to the fog, to whatever she saw in the glass that Wren could not. "That is the choice that interests me. Not what you copied. Not what you saw. The choice to return to the place where the records are kept, when you could have stayed in the dark and let the night finish without you."

The harbor bell began to ring, distant and slow, counting an hour that Wren had lost track of. The fog did not lift. The lamp burned on the desk between them, and the Salt Ledgers waited on their shelves, patient as the sea, containing every entry that had ever been made and every entry that ever would be, if the balance held.
