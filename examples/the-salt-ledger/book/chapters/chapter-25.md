## Chapter Text
Wren did not walk on to berth nine. She did not turn back for the Customs House door. She went to the harbor wall, not to the plaque but to the iron bollards below it, where the fishing smacks used to tie up before the new quay was built. The third bollard from the end had a hollow core — she had pressed her ear to it as a child, listening to the tide echo inside the iron, and her father had laughed and told her the sea was talking back. She slid the roll into the hollow, pushing it deep where rust had flaked away and the darkness went further than her fingers could follow. The docket's edge caught, then released. She stood and walked away without looking back, her hands empty, her coat flat against her sides.

The fog took her. She walked inland, away from the quay, letting the cobbles guide her feet. Without the roll against her ribs she felt lighter than she should have, as if she had left her own heartbeat in the bollard along with the paper. Seventy-six quarters in the dark, her own slanted sevens among them, the heron seal repeated like a pulse. Let someone else find it. Let someone else ask.

The lanes narrowed. The salt cod smell faded, replaced by the inland smell of wet earth and closed bakeries. She was going to disappear. She would walk until the road forked toward the towns inland, until Harrowmere was a name she had left behind with the dead ship and the living lie. She would be the clerk who had vanished, another gap in the record that no one would think to question.

Then she saw the light.

It was not a street lamp. Those were yellow and steady, spaced along the quay at intervals she knew by heart. This light was white, faint, moving — a lantern on water, or on a deck, or in a hand. It came from the north, from beyond the fish market, from the direction of berth nine.

She stopped. The fog swallowed the light, then gave it back. Closer now, or further away — the fog made distance meaningless. But she knew what she was seeing. The lamp was lit. Someone had lit it, or someone was lighting it now, in answer to a message written in pencil on a docket nineteen years ago: *Tell O. the lamp is lit.*

The ship was not coming tomorrow night. The ship was already here.

Or someone was preparing for it. Or the lamp was always lit, had been lit for nineteen years, and she had simply never walked this way in fog thick enough to make it visible.

Wren stood at the edge of the inland road with the harbor behind her and the lamp ahead of her, and understood that she could not disappear while the light burned. The roll was safe in the bollard, or as safe as anything hidden in iron and rust could be. But she was still here, empty-handed, with the whole night before her and a light moving in the dark that said the lie was not finished with her yet.
