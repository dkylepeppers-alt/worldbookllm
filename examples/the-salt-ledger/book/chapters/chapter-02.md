## Beat

Wren hides the docket and returns to the ledger room to copy more evidence, but the watchman's rounds force her to choose between thoroughness, escape, or concealment.

## Choices

- **Copy every quarter before leaving** (a new beat): thoroughness — Wren has the full nineteen-year record but the watchman finds the ledger room disturbed; her presence is known
- **Gather what she has and go** (a new beat): caution — Wren carries partial evidence home but the cellar box gap and open ledger room remain; she has enough to understand the pattern but not to prove it

## Chapter Text
Wren folded the docket into her coat with the copied sheet, her fingers clumsy with hurry. The cellar box gaped open, one file fewer than it should hold, and she did not have time to rearrange the rest to hide the absence. She blew out her lamp and took the stairs two at a time, her boots loud on the stone, emerging into the corridor just as the watchman's bell rang the quarter-hour somewhere above.

The ledger room was dark. She had left it that way, but now the darkness felt different—like a held breath. She relit her lamp from the corridor sconce and shut the door behind her, sliding the bolt with a click that seemed to carry through the whole floor.

She had the docket. She had her own copied sheet. What she did not have was enough.

The volumes stood on their shelf in the order she had left them. Wren pulled down the first of the nineteen years and opened it on the desk, her lamp turned low. The pages were cold and slightly stiff, the ink faded to brown in the older entries. She found the first _Margery Lane_ line—spring quarter, nineteen years ago, in a clerk's hand she did not recognize, the heron seal already crisp and new—and copied it onto a second loose sheet. Then the next quarter. Then the next. The names of the clerks changed as the years turned, a roster of the Customs House's forgotten juniors, each one copying the line without asking, each seal placed by the same hand that wore the heron ring.

Her own name appeared on the eighth sheet. She copied it anyway, her pen moving steadily, as if the act of transcription could separate her from the girl who had written the original.

The harbor bell rang once, twice, three times. She had lost count of the quarters. Her hand ached. The pile of copied sheets grew, each one a quarter-year of a ship that did not exist, and beneath her coat the docket pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

A floorboard creaked in the corridor outside.

Wren held her breath. The sound did not repeat, but she knew the watchman's rounds: up the main stair, along the corridor past the assessors' offices, then the back stair down to the cellars. She had perhaps ten minutes if he was running late, five if he was not. The current volume was still open on the desk, her pen uncapped, the copied sheets scattered where she had set them to dry.

She could stay and finish what she had started. She could gather what she already had and trust the partial record to be enough. Or she could find some corner of the room to hide it all, leave with nothing in her hands, and hope that empty hands looked like innocence if the watchman turned his lamp her way.
