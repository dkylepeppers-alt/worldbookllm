## Beat

Wren interrupts Varga's origin story to demand what she wants from her now, forcing Varga to admit she has been improvising quarter by quarter for nineteen years without a true plan, and that she no longer knows whether she wants to be stopped, helped to finish, or replaced.

- **Demand the answer again** (a new beat): confrontation — Varga must answer whether she has been improvising or planning, and what role she imagines for Wren in ending or continuing the arrangement
- **Ask who else could have stopped this** (a new beat): investigation — Wren shifts the frame from Varga's guilt to the system's complicity, asking about the other clerks, the Tidewardens, and whether anyone else could be made to answer

## Chapter Text
"What do you want from me?" Wren asked.

The words cut through Varga's measured cadence like a knife through ledger paper. Varga's mouth closed on whatever sentence she had been shaping. The heron ring stilled on her finger.

The stove in the corner had cooled to ticking silence. Outside, the fog pressed against the window glass with the weight of water held in suspension, and the harbor bell had stopped counting, as if the whole harbor waited to hear what Varga would say next.

For a long moment, nothing. Varga's hands remained flat on the blotter, the heron ring facing upward, a grey eye in the lamplight. Her shoulders held their stiffness. Then, slowly, her fingers curled inward, the ring disappearing into her palm, and she looked at Wren with an expression Wren had never seen on her face before—not command, not assessment, but something perilously close to need.

"I want—" Varga began, and stopped. The sentence hung incomplete, a ledger entry with no figure in the final column. She tried again. "I have not been asked what I want in nineteen years. I have been asked whether the account balances. Whether the seal is clear. Whether the payment arrived on time."

"I'm asking now."

Varga's jaw tightened. She looked away, toward the window where the fog erased the harbor, and when she spoke her voice had lost some of its careful placement, some of the weight she gave each word to make it land precisely. "Tomorrow night, a ship will come to berth nine. It will not be the Margery Lane—the Margery Lane is at the bottom of the Teeth, and I do not know what name it sails under now. A man who calls himself Cask will sign the docket, or someone who has learned to copy his signature well enough to fool a clerk who is not looking closely. The tithe will be paid. The lamp will be extinguished. The harbor account will balance."

She turned back to face Wren. "That is what happens. That is what has happened seventy-six times. I do not know what I want because I stopped wanting things in terms that could be answered. I wanted the columns to add up. They add up. I wanted no one to ask why. No one asked. Until you."

Wren felt the pressure of the desk edge against her ribs, the familiar geometry of a room she had believed she understood. "You had a plan when Cask came to your door. You agreed to something."

"I was twenty-four years old. I was alone in a rented room with a ring my mother had left me and a position that could be taken away by anyone with a cousin in the harbor office. Cask offered me money and silence, and I took them because I could not imagine a future where refusing would lead anywhere I wanted to go." Varga's voice had dropped almost to a whisper, but the whisper carried more force than her measured tones had. "I did not plan nineteen years. I planned one quarter, and then another, and then the quarter after that, until planning became habit and habit became structure and the structure became—" She stopped. Her hand opened, the heron ring visible again, dull in the lamplight. "This. What you see. What I am."

Wren watched her. The woman who had taught her to check her sevens, to weight each column twice, to believe that attention mattered. The woman who had pressed seventy-six heron seals in grey wax and never once looked away while doing it. The fog moved against the glass, patient, indifferent, carrying tomorrow night's tide toward a berth where a lamp would be lit and a ship would come in under a dead name.

"And now?" Wren asked. "Now that someone has asked?"

Varga's eyes met hers. The need was still there, almost hidden beneath the exhaustion, but present—a current running beneath ice. "Now I find I do not know whether I want to be stopped or to be helped to finish. Whether I want you to expose what I have done or to understand why I did it and choose to continue it yourself. Whether—" She stopped again. Her hand closed on the ring, a gesture Wren had seen her make a hundred times when reviewing a difficult account, as if the heron could tell her what the figures could not.

"Whether you want a clerk's absolution," Wren said, "or a clerk's complicity."

The lamplight flickered. The fog pressed. And somewhere in the dark between the harbor bell's last stroke and the next, Varga's shoulders dropped a fraction, the stiffness finally loosening into something that might have been grief or might have been relief, and she did not deny it.
