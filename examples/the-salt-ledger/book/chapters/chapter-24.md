## Chapter Text
The fog was thicker going back. Wren kept her hand on the harbor wall until her fingers found the iron gate of the Customs House service entrance, the one the watchman used when he did not want to climb the front steps. It was unlocked, as it always was, and she passed through into the narrow passage that led to the cellars.

The corridor smelled of brine and lamp oil, the same cellar smell, but upstairs now, closer to where people worked and slept. She did not go to the ledger room. Varga would not be there. Wren went down, her boots finding the same stairs she had climbed an hour before, the roll of paper shifting against her ribs with each step.

The cellar door stood open. Lamplight moved on the wall beyond it, slow and steady, not the flicker of a draft but the deliberate motion of someone walking with a lamp held low. Wren stopped on the threshold.

Varga stood among the docket boxes with her back to the door. She had opened the autumn quarter box — the same box, the same gap near the bottom where the _Margery Lane_ docket had lain. Her grey coat hung straight to her heels. Her right hand, the one with the heron ring, rested on the crate's edge.

"I took it," Wren said.

Varga did not turn. "I know. I counted."

"Then you know what else I have."

Varga's hand moved, a small gesture, nothing Wren could read. "Seventy-six quarters. You copied them all. I wondered whether you would stop at your own name, or keep going until the hand that wrote the first one."

"I kept going."

"Yes." Varga turned. In the lamplight her face was the same face Wren had studied for three years — the slight asymmetry of her eyebrows, the way she held her mouth when she was deciding whether a mistake was worth mentioning — but the wear in it was deeper now, visible in a way that daylight and distance had always hidden. "You would. That was the thing I could never predict, Wren. Whether your patience was a virtue or a trap you had set for yourself."

Wren came into the cellar. The water moved under the floor, the same whisper she had heard before, and she understood that Varga had come back here not to check on the docket but to wait. The open box, the counted files, the lamp held low — these were the preparations of someone who had already decided what she would say when the door opened.

"Who is O.?" Wren asked again.

"Someone who pays what he owes." Varga's voice was quiet, the same quiet she used in the assessment room, but here in the cellar it did not carry the same weight. Here it sounded like a woman speaking to herself, testing whether the words still held. "Someone who was on the _Margery Lane_ when she went out past the Teeth, and who came back when the others did not. Someone I promised that the harbor would not forget him, if he kept paying for the memory."

Wren felt the roll of paper against her side, seventy-six quarters of memory, her own hand among them. "The plaque says eleven men."

"The plaque says what the sea left." Varga looked at the open box, at the gap where the docket had been. "O. Cask was the master. He was also the only one who could swim."

The cellar was very still. The harbor bell rang, distant and muffled, counting a tide that had turned while they stood there.

"You have a choice," Varga said. "You have always had a choice, from the moment you found the first entry. The others did not see, or they saw and chose not to understand. You understood. And now you must decide whether understanding is enough, or whether you need to be the one who ends it."

She stepped away from the crate. The lamplight fell between them, a bright space on the damp stone floor, and Wren saw that Varga's hands were empty, the heron ring catching the light, and that she had made no move to take the roll or close the box or call for the watchman. She was waiting. She had been waiting, perhaps, since the night nineteen years ago when she first pressed her seal into grey wax and gave a dead ship a reason to keep sailing.
