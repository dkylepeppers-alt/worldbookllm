## Chapter Text
Wren shifted her weight onto the balls of her feet, the roll of paper a hard cylinder against her ribs. The woman in oilskins stood with her back to the warehouse, facing the black water where the fog moved like a living thing. The lamp burned at her feet, steady and patient, saying nothing.

She could not say what moved her. Perhaps it was the woman's stillness, the quality of waiting that asked nothing and expected nothing. Perhaps it was the weight of seventy-six quarters in her coat, the copied names of clerks who had done exactly what Wren had done: written the line, sealed the ledger, gone home to sleep. Perhaps it was simply that she had come this far to look, and looking had changed nothing, and someone had to make the first gesture toward whatever came next.

She drew the roll from inside her coat. The blotter cord was damp from her body heat and the fog. She held it in both hands for a moment, feeling the edges of the copied sheets through the paper, the docket with its pencilled message pressed somewhere in the middle. Then she set it down at the corner of the warehouse wall, where the stone met the quay, and pushed it forward with her boot until it rested in the small space of clearer ground between the crab traps and the lamp's circle of light.

The roll sat there, grey and anonymous, no different from any discarded docket or dropped manifest. But the woman in oilskins turned.

She did not startle. She turned the way someone turns when a harbor bell changes its pattern, a shift in the quality of attention rather than a surprise. Her eyes found the roll immediately, then traced the line of the warehouse wall to the darkness where Wren crouched. She did not raise her voice. She did not reach for the roll.

"First clerk," she said, and the words were not quite a question. Her voice was low, rough with salt, the voice of someone who spent her hours in wind rather than in rooms. "First clerk to come see what the writing meant."

Wren did not answer. The woman looked at the roll again, then at the lamp, then back at the darkness where Wren waited.

"You left it where I'd find it," she said. "That means you want me to do something with it. Or you want to see what I do."

The tide whispered against the quay. The fog moved between them, uncertain. The woman knelt and touched the roll with one finger, not unrolling it, only acknowledging its weight and its presence. Her hands were broad and scarred, the hands of someone who worked with rope and oar.

"Nineteen years," she said, "and the only ones who came were the ones who were told to come. The ones with the seal already on their orders. You're not one of those."

She stood, leaving the roll where it lay, and turned back to face the water. The lamp burned between them, a small steady flame that made the fog glow amber where it passed through.

"I'll tend the lamp," she said. "You decide if you want what's in that roll to be what I find, or what you keep. I'll be here when the tide turns again."

She did not look back. The arrangement held, patient and unbroken, and Wren stood at the edge of the light with empty hands and the harbor bell counting hours she could no longer name.
