## Chapter Text
Wren did not let the silence settle. She had heard Varga's story—the man at the door, the seawater in his hair, the offer of balance in exchange for complicity—and she understood now that it was a story Varga had told herself for nineteen years, polished like a stone in a tide-pool until its edges no longer cut. But understanding the story was not the same as accepting it.

"Why you?" Wren asked. The words came out sharp, the edge of a clerk who had spent hours checking columns and finding the gaps where figures did not align. "There were other assessors. Older ones. The Customs House had clerks with more authority, more to lose, more to gain. Why did Cask come to a woman with a small room above the chandlery and a reputation for keeping accounts?"

Varga's hands stilled on the blotter. The heron ring caught the flame and held it, steady, but something in her face adjusted—a tightening around the eyes, a minute compression of the lips that Wren had learned to read during three years of corrections and marginal notes.

"I asked myself that," Varga said. "For the first year, for the second. I thought perhaps he had observed me, perhaps he had heard of my work, perhaps—" She stopped. The silence that followed was different from the one before: not the held breath of confession, but the pause of someone who had reached the end of a prepared statement and found the next page blank.

"No," Varga said, and the word was small, almost swallowed by the stove's ticking. "No. He came to me because I was junior enough to need what he offered, and careful enough to make it work. Because I was alone. Because I was young enough to believe that a ledger that balanced was the same thing as justice."

Wren felt the answer land. It had the quality of truth—not the polished truth of the story, but the raw truth underneath, the figure that appeared when you carried the subtraction one column further than you had meant to go.

"And the first payment," Wren said. "Triple tithe. What did it buy? Not the ship—the ship was already at the bottom of the Teeth. Not the cargo—the copper was already lost. What did Cask purchase with that first triple payment?"

Varga's jaw tightened. For a moment the controlled mask held, the Master Assessor who had faced clerks and shipmasters and harbor inspectors for nineteen years without flinching. Then something cracked—not dramatically, not with tears or raised voice, but with the subtle collapse of a structure that had borne weight too long.

"Silence," Varga said. "He bought my silence. Not the harbor's—the harbor didn't know to speak. Not the ledger's—the ledger only records, it doesn't accuse. He bought my silence, specifically, personally, and I sold it because I was afraid that if I refused, someone else would agree, and I would be left with nothing but the knowledge that I had been offered a choice and found myself too principled to take it."

The words hung between them, heavier than the earlier confession. Wren sat in her chair and felt the desk edge press against her ribs and understood that she was hearing something Varga had never said aloud, perhaps had never admitted even to herself in the quiet hours between sealing a docket and sleeping with the heron ring still on her hand.

"Do you still believe it?" Wren asked. "That balance was worth more than honesty?"

Varga looked at her. The lamplight showed everything now: the grey beneath the eyes, the fine lines that had not been there nineteen years ago, the slight tremor in the fingers that had pressed seventy-six seals. She did not answer immediately. The harbor bell rang, distant and slow, and the fog pressed against the glass, and the two of them sat in the small circle of light while nineteen years of balanced ledgers waited for a verdict.

"I believed it the first quarter," Varga said finally. "I believed it the fifth, the tenth. I believed it because the alternative was to admit that I had sold my silence for a column that added up, and that the men who died on the Margery Lane had been worth exactly three times the usual salt tithe, payable in advance."

She stopped. Her hands closed into fists on the blotter, then opened again, slowly, the heron ring facing upward like a seal waiting for wax.

"I don't know when I stopped believing. Perhaps the twentieth quarter, perhaps the fortieth. Perhaps only tonight, when you walked in with empty hands and asked your questions not with outrage but with—" She searched for the word. "With patience. The patience of someone who sees what others skim. I told you not to stop, Miss Adair. I did not expect you to apply that instruction to me."

The confession was complete now, or as complete as any confession ever was. Wren sat in her chair and felt the weight of what she had extracted: not evidence, not proof, but the admission that the woman who had taught her to look carefully had stopped believing her own justification long ago, and had continued anyway because stopping would have meant admitting that nineteen years of balance were also nineteen years of error, compounded quarterly, accumulating interest in a currency of silence and sealed wax.

The stove ticked once, twice. Somewhere below, the tide turned in the cellar, pressing against foundations that had grown soft with years of brine. Wren looked at her own hands—small, ink-stained, the hands of a clerk who had copied the Margery Lane entry without knowing what it meant—and understood that the next question would define what happened when the fog lifted. She could press further into the past, or she could pull Varga into the present, or she could offer something of her own to match what Varga had laid on the blotter between them.
