## Chapter Text
Wren's pen did not stop. The floorboard creaked again, closer this time, a heavy tread measured and regular — the watchman's boots, not Varga's lighter step. She copied the winter quarter of the third year back, the clerk's name smeared where damp had got at the page, and moved to the next volume without looking up.

The sheets covered the desk now, a fan of grey paper each bearing the same lie in a different hand. Her own name appeared twice more as she worked, years she did not remember, fog quarters and summer quarters, her slanted sevens marching across the page in service of a ship that had never come in. She copied them too. The act had become something beyond evidence-gathering: a penance, an accounting, a refusal to let any of it stay hidden.

The footsteps stopped outside the door.

Wren held her breath. The bolt was shot; she had shot it herself, but the watchman had keys to every door in the Customs House. She heard the jingle of the ring, the scrape of iron in the lock, and then silence — a long pause in which she could hear her own heartbeat and the harbor bell marking some fraction of the tide she had lost track of.

The lock did not turn. Perhaps he was listening. Perhaps he had already heard the scratch of her pen through the wood and was waiting to see if the sound would repeat. Wren sat perfectly still, the uncapped pen drying in her fingers, the open ledger before her a confession in plain sight.

The footsteps moved on. Not toward the stairs — toward the assessors' offices, the other wing, his rounds continuing in their ordained pattern. She had minutes, perhaps less.

She wrote faster. The last three years, the last two, the final quarter that brought her to the autumn docket already folded in her coat. She did not stop to read what she copied. The names of the clerks blurred together, a nineteen-year procession of juniors who had sat where she sat and seen what she saw and written the line and thought nothing of it. She was finishing the last sheet when she heard the watchman's bell ring the three-quarter hour from the floor below.

She had the complete record. Seventy-six quarters, seventy-six entries, seventy-six heron seals in grey wax. The sheets would not fold small; she had to roll them and tie them with the cord from the blotter, a cylinder of paper thick as her wrist. The docket joined them, the pencilled message _Tell O. the lamp is lit_ pressed against the copied names of clerks long gone.

The harbor-side door was her only way out. The main stairs would take her past the watchman's station; the back stairs led down to the cellars where Varga had found her once already. The fog had come in while she worked, swallowing the lamps of the lower harbor. She stood with the roll of paper in her hands and the door before her, the bolt already drawn back, and knew that whatever she did next would not be undone.

The roll was heavy. It contained every quarter of the lie, her own hand among them, the heron seal repeated like a pulse. She could carry it out into the fog and let the harbor decide what she was. She could hide it in the wall cavity where old heating pipes had left a space behind the wainscoting, and leave with empty hands that knew where the truth waited. Or she could set it down on the desk, open to the page where her own name appeared, and sit in the lamplight until the watchman's key turned in the lock and someone official had to say, out loud, what happened to clerks who asked what the sea had kept.
