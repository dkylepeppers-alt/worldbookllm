## Beat

Wren walks into the harbor fog with the full nineteen-year record, reaching the edge of the fish market where berth nine waits in darkness, and must choose where to take the evidence before morning finds her missing.

## Choices

- **Hide the roll and disappear** (a new beat): sacrifice — she preserves the evidence at the cost of her own presence, becoming a missing clerk by morning

## Chapter Text
The harbor door opened onto fog. Not the thin mist of autumn evenings but a true harbor fog, thick enough to swallow footsteps and deaden the bell buoys out on the Teeth. Wren pulled her collar up and stepped through, the roll of paper heavy against her side where she had tucked it inside her coat, tied with the blotter cord and pressing the docket's edge into her ribs.

The door clicked shut behind her. She did not look back. The north quay ran left toward the fish market and berth nine; the main harbor ran right, where the larger ships tied up and the watchman's lantern made a pale smear against the grey. She went left, keeping to the wall, her boots finding the gaps between cobbles by memory. The fog smelled of salt cod and cold stone and the particular damp of a tide that had turned without her noticing.

She had the complete record. Seventy-six quarters, every clerk's name, every heron seal, her own hand repeated across the years like a fingerprint she could not erase. The weight of it was physical. The roll shifted as she walked, and she found herself holding her elbow against it as if someone might try to take it from her, though the quay was empty and the fish market shuttered, the awnings furled and dripping.

A Tidewarden boat creaked at its mooring somewhere ahead. She could not see it yet, but she knew the sound: the short knock of a hull against fenders, the rattle of a shroud in a breeze too faint to feel. The Tidewardens kept their boats at berth nine, past the market, past the last of the working quay. Nobody brought a ship in there. The docket said otherwise. The docket bore the heron seal, and someone had written in pencil: _Tell O. the lamp is lit._

Wren stopped at the corner where the market ended and the quay narrowed. The fog was thicker here, or perhaps the lamps were fewer. She could see her own hand in front of her face, the fingers that had copied seventy-six entries and written three of the originals, but not much beyond. The sound of the boat was closer. Not moving—moored, waiting, occupied or empty she could not tell.

She thought of the plaque on the harbor wall, the one her father had shown her when she was small. Eleven men. O. Cask. The sea did this to people who were in a hurry. She had never been in a hurry before tonight. She had always been the clerk who went slowly, who checked twice, who believed that the reason was written down if you were willing to look.

Now she had looked, and the reason was in her coat, and the fog offered no advice about what to do with it. The tide whispered against the quay stones. Somewhere in the dark a lamp was lit, or would be lit, or had been lit for nineteen years while clerks like her copied the line and thought nothing of it.

She stood at the corner of the fish market with the complete record heavy against her ribs and the whole night still before her. The harbor bell began to count, distant and muffled, measuring something she could no longer name.
