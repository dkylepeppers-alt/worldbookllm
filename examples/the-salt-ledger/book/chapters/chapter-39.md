## Chapter Text
She chose the dark beside a stack of empty crab traps, settling where the warehouse wall met the quay at an angle that hid her from the lamp while letting her see both the flame and the boat. The fog clung to her hair and found the gaps in her collar. The roll of paper inside her coat pressed against her ribs whenever she breathed too deep, a reminder of why she was crouched on wet stone instead of asleep in her lodging.

Time moved differently at berth nine. The harbor bell rang twice, three times, each stroke muffled and directionless in the fog. The lamp burned on, its flame steady as a held breath, consuming oil at a rate Wren began to measure against the passing hours. Someone had filled it recently. Someone tended it.

The tide turned. She heard it in the change of the water against the quay, a shift from slap to suck, the boat rising slightly on its lines. Her legs ached. She shifted her weight by inches, her boots finding purchase on kelp-slick stone. The fog thinned and thickened without pattern, offering glimpses of the masthead, the empty quay, the black water beyond.

It was nearly the fourth bell when the figure came.

Wren saw the light first—a second lamp, moving, swinging in a hand that kept it low. The figure walked without hurry, a woman's shape in oilskins and a knitted cap, her boots making no sound on the stone. She went straight to the quayside lamp and knelt, checking the wick with a practiced motion, her face caught for a moment in the doubled light.

Wren knew her. She had seen her once on the quay, months ago, arguing with a weigher about a cargo of dried cod. A Tidewarden. Not one of the pilots who brought the big ships through the Teeth, but one of the boat-keepers, the ones who maintained the craft that carried the pilots out to waiting hulls. Her name was something common—Mara, Maren, a harbor name that Wren could not quite summon.

The woman did not look toward the crab traps. She finished with the lamp and stepped onto the boat, her weight settling it lower in the water. Wren heard her moving in the dark, a block shifting, a line being readied. Not departure preparations—not yet. The readiness of someone who expected to cast off on a signal, or at a particular hour, or when a particular shape emerged from the fog.

She emerged again and stood on the quay, facing the water, her back to Wren and the warehouse. The lamp beside her did not flicker. The fog moved through the space between them like a third presence, uncertain which side to settle on.

Wren understood the rhythm then, watching the woman's stillness. This was not a smuggler's nervous vigil, waiting for the watchman's round to pass. This was a duty, old and accepted, the kind of waiting that became its own form of patience. Nineteen years of tending a lamp that no official eye ever checked, of readying a boat that no ledger named, of standing in fog that swallowed everything except the knowledge that the arrangement held.

The woman checked the sky, though there was nothing to see but grey. She checked the boat again. She did not speak, did not whistle, did not make any sound that might carry across water. The silence was the signal. The silence and the lamp, burning where no lamp needed to burn, saying without words that berth nine was open and the Teeth would be guided.

Wren's knee throbbed where the stone pressed against it. She had been watching for hours, and she had learned what observation could teach: the face of one keeper, the readiness of one boat, the exact quality of light that said the arrangement lived. She did not know if a ship would come tonight, or if the lamp was lit every night, or if tomorrow's tide was the true appointment. She knew only that someone had kept this burning for nineteen years, and that the woman in oilskins had done it long enough to move without fear of discovery.

The fog thickened again, swallowing the boat, swallowing the woman's shape until she was only a darker patch against the dark, standing beside the steady flame. The harbor bell began to ring, distant and slow, counting an hour Wren had lost track of. She was cold deep in her chest, the kind of cold that came from stillness and damp and the weight of paper against her heart.

She could stay. She could wait for whatever the lamp summoned, and see it with her own eyes, and know the complete shape of the lie she had uncovered. She could step forward now, announce herself, ask the woman in oilskins what name she gave to the work she did. Or she could slip back into the fog the way she had come, carrying the complete record and the face of one keeper, and decide in a warmer place what justice looked like when it had been kept burning by patience rather than malice.
