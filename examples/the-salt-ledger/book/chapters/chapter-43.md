## Chapter Text
Wren rose from beside the crab traps.

Her knee buckled, numb from hours against the stone, and she caught herself on the warehouse wall. The sound was small — a boot scraping kelp — but in the stillness of berth nine it carried. The woman by the lamp turned.

Maren. The name came to Wren fully now, the harbor name she had almost summoned while watching from the dark. The keeper's face was caught in the lamplight, older than the fog had made her seem, broad hands still raised from checking the wick. She did not reach for anything. She did not call out. She stood with the patience of someone who had learned that most interruptions passed if you did not chase them.

Wren stepped into the light.

The flame warmed her face where the cold had settled deep. She felt the roll of paper inside her coat, seventy-six quarters tied with blotter cord, pressing against her ribs. She had watched this woman for hours, measuring oil against the bells, reading the readiness of the boat, understanding the rhythm of a duty that asked no questions. Now she stood where that rhythm could see her, and the rhythm paused, and assessed, and waited.

"You've been there since the third bell," Maren said. Not a question. A fact, the way a weigher might note the weight of a catch.

"Before that."

"I know. I smelled the lamp oil on the air when I came." Maren's hands dropped to her sides. "I thought you'd go home when the cold bit deep enough. Most would."

"I'm not most clerks."

"No." The boat creaked at its mooring. "Most clerks copy the line and think nothing of it. You came to see what the writing meant."

"I came because I wrote the line myself."

Maren's head tilted, the same assessment Wren had seen her give the sky when she checked for ships that did not come. "Then you know what it cost."

"I know what it cost me. I don't know what it cost you."

The fog moved between them in currents Wren could not predict. The lamp burned on, and now she stood close enough to see the wick's blackened tip, the thin curl of smoke, the small imperfections of a signal tended by human hands for nineteen years.

Maren looked at the bulge beneath Wren's coat. Her eyes were not unkind. They were the eyes of someone who had learned to read the harbor in darkness, who had stood at berth nine through nights worse than this and had not asked for company.

"You've got something in there," Maren said. "More than yourself. More than cold."

Wren did not reach for the roll. To touch it now would be to declare the shape of her evidence, to transform from watcher into participant, to answer the question Maren had not asked: what do you mean to do with what you know?

The boat rocked on its lines. The harbor bell rang once, distant, marking an hour that belonged to neither night nor morning. Wren stood in the lamplight with the complete record heavy against her heart and the keeper's eyes on her face, and understood that she had crossed from observation into encounter. The hours in the dark had taught her how the arrangement lived. Now she would learn what it meant to be seen by it.
