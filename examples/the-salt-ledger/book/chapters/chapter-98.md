## Beat

Wren stands and leaves the Customs House without another word, returning to the fog and the harbor; Varga must decide whether to let her go, follow her, or prepare for whatever Wren might do with what she knows, and Wren must face the night alone with knowledge that has no proof behind it.

## Choices

- **Return to the Customs House and sit at her desk** (a new beat): confront — Wren forces Varga's hand by making herself visible in Varga's own territory, risking that Varga will seal the ledger against her or use her presence to frame her as complicit.
- **Return to berth nine and ask Maren to douse the lamp** (a new beat): persuade — Wren seeks to convert Maren's grief into action by asking her to extinguish the lamp, risking that Maren will refuse and Wren will have burned her only ally, or that Maren will agree and the arrangement will collapse with unknown consequences for everyone involved.
- **Find Varga and offer to take her place** (a new beat): comply — Wren chooses to become part of the system she uncovered, offering to take over the ledger entries and learn the full mechanism of the arrangement, trading her moral clarity for Varga's trust and the power to understand what she cannot yet stop.

## Chapter Text
The chair scraped behind her. Wren stood.

She did not look at Varga again. She did not trust what her face might show, or what Varga's might ask of her. The ledger room was warm, too warm, the stove banking high against a fog that had already swallowed the quay and the fish market and the lamp at berth nine. She walked past the shelves where nineteen years of Salt Ledgers stood in their rows, each volume balanced, each column adding up to a number that was real even when the ship that produced it was not.

The doorframe passed. The corridor. The stairs downward, each step worn in the center by clerks who had never asked why a dead brigantine still paid its salt. Her boots found the worn centers automatically. The fog met her at the harbor door, colder than she remembered, carrying the smell of mudflats and the deeper rot of the harbor at low tide.

She did not run. Running would have meant looking back to see if Varga followed, and she would not give her that. She walked into the fog the way she had walked out of it hours before, one hand on the warehouse wall, letting her boots find the gaps between cobbles. The harbor bell rang somewhere to her left, counting an hour she could not name, the sound flat and directionless in the grey.

Behind her, the Customs House stood with its lamp burning in the ledger room window. She did not know if Varga watched from the glass. She did not know if Varga had already moved to the drawer where the heron ring waited, or to the cellar where the first docket lay in its box, or to the door above the chandlery where a man with seawater in his hair had stood nineteen years ago and made an offer that sounded like safety.

She knew only that she walked alone now, with a story in her head and nothing in her hands. The knowledge was sharp and useless, a blade without a handle. She could tell the watchman, but what would she show him? She could tell the Tidewardens, but Maren already knew. She could tell the harbor, but the harbor had heard this story every quarter for nineteen years and had chosen not to listen.

The quay widened. She could see the watchman's lantern now, a pale smear against the grey, and beyond it the darker shapes of ships at mooring. The Margery Lane was not among them. The Margery Lane was at the bottom of the Teeth, or in the ledger, or somewhere on the other side of the water where a lamp still burned and someone still believed the signal meant safe passage.

Wren stopped at the corner where the north quay met the harbor proper. The fog moved through the space between buildings, carrying the smell of the sea and the weight of hours she could not reclaim. She had left Varga sitting at her desk with a confession unfinished and a ring still turning on her finger. She had left Maren at berth nine with seventy-six quarters of copied record and a lamp that would burn until someone told her to stop. She had left herself with a story that balanced the way Varga's columns balanced: every figure in its place, every debit matched by a credit, the whole thing adding up to a number that was real and a truth that was not.

The tide whispered against the stone. Tomorrow night's ship would come regardless of where Wren stood when it arrived. The question was whether she would be there to see it, whether she would let the arrangement continue its patient unbroken course, or whether she would find some way to make her witness matter without the evidence she had given away.

She stood at the corner, empty-handed, and waited for the fog to tell her which direction led somewhere she could still stand to be.
