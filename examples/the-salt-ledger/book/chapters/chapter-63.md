## Chapter Text
Wren knelt. The fog had dampened the quay stones enough to make the paper cling, and she weighted each corner with a pebble from the gap between the blocks—grey stone on grey paper, the harbor providing its own seals. The roll opened slowly, the first sheet springing flat, the last resisting, memory in the fiber wanting to curl back into itself.

Seventy-six quarters. She laid them in a row, a timeline of ink and wax crawling toward Maren's boots.

Maren did not kneel immediately. She stood with her oilskins dripping faintly onto the stone, her pale eyes moving from sheet to sheet, reading the progression without touching it. The early hands were strange to her, Wren could tell—the careful copperplate of clerks long gone, the slanted hurried script of a man who had left the Customs House for a fever ward, the rounded letters of a woman who had married out to a fishing village east of the Teeth. Then the hands began to repeat, the same names cycling back as juniors became seniors and new juniors took their places, and Maren's jaw tightened.

"Here." Wren pointed to the eighth sheet, not touching it, only showing. Her own sevens, slanted, careful. The autumn quarter, two years past. "And here." The spring quarter before that. "And here." The winter quarter that had followed her mother's letter about the cough.

Maren knelt. Her scarred hands hovered over the sheets as if they were hot, then settled on either side of the row, framing it. She did not read aloud. Her lips moved slightly, forming names she had never needed to know—names of clerks who had copied the line and sealed the ledger and gone home to sleep, names of the living who had served the dead without knowing it.

Her finger found the first heron seal, nineteen years past. Crisp, new, the wax still carrying the fine detail of a seal that had since worn soft with repetition. She traced it to the next quarter, the next, the next. The progression of the seal told its own story: the heron standing on one leg, gradually less distinct, gradually more hurried, as if the hand that pressed it had grown tired of the gesture or confident that no one would look closely enough to see the wear.

"She used the same ring," Maren said. Not a question.

"Every quarter."

"Every quarter." Maren's finger stopped on Wren's third entry, the spring quarter of last year. "And you."

"And me."

The silence that followed was not the silence of the fog or the tide. It was the silence of something held too long, a breath that had forgotten how to be released. Maren's shoulders moved once, a shudder that might have been cold and might have been something else, and when she looked up from the sheets her pale eyes were wet.

"I thought it was one lamp," she said. "One boat. One tide. I thought—" She stopped. Her hand closed on the edge of the sheet nearest her, the paper crumpling slightly under her grip. "Nineteen years. Do you know how many times I've filled that lamp? How many times I've checked the wick and trimmed it and set it burning again? I thought I was keeping a promise. I thought the promise was small enough to hold in one hand."

Wren said nothing. The sheets lay between them, a river of ink and wax and names, and she saw what she had made by unrolling it: not evidence, not proof, but a mirror. Maren looked into it and saw the true size of what she had tended.

"Eleven men," Maren said. "That's what the plaque says. Eleven men and a hold full of copper. But this—" She gestured at the row of sheets, the seventy-six quarters, the heron seal repeated like a heartbeat. "This says something else. This says the Margery Lane never stopped sailing. This says someone has been paying for the dead to stay buried, and I've been lighting the lamp that tells them it's safe to come in."

She stood abruptly, the movement sending one sheet skittering across the stone. Wren caught it—a summer quarter, five years past, a clerk's name she did not recognize. She set it back in its place, weighting it again with the pebble, and rose to face Maren.

The woman in oilskins had turned toward the water. The lamp at her feet still burned, steady and patient, but its light seemed smaller now against the scale of what lay spread on the quay. The fog moved through the space between them, indifferent, carrying the smell of the sea and the distant sound of a bell buoy clanging somewhere past the Teeth.

"Tomorrow night's tide," Maren said, her back to Wren. "The ship will come. It always comes when the lamp is lit. I don't know what it carries. I don't know who sails it. I know only that someone on the other side still believes the signal means safe passage, and that your mentor's seal clears the ledger, and that the harbor account balances because money moves through a dead ship's name."

She turned. Her face was wet now, the tears tracking salt down cheeks that had known worse weather than fog. "What do you want, Wren Adair? You brought me this. You laid it out where I had to see it. What do you want me to do with seventy-six quarters of a light I thought was keeping people safe?"

The question hung between them, heavier than the roll of paper had been in Wren's coat. The tide whispered against the quay. The lamp burned. And somewhere in the fog, the arrangement waited for tomorrow night's ship, patient and undisturbed, unless someone chose to disturb it.
