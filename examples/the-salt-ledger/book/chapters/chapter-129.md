## Chapter Text
Wren's fingers moved.

They did not hesitate. They moved the way they moved when she reached for a pen she knew was there, when she turned to a fresh page and began a new entry without looking at the old. She closed her own hand around Varga's fist—not hard, not gently, but with the pressure of a clerk who had finally found the figure that belonged at the bottom of the column.

Varga's knuckles were cold beneath her palm. The heron ring pressed against Wren's fingertips through the thin skin of Varga's hand, a hard silver edge that had pressed seventy-six times into grey wax. For a moment neither woman breathed. The lamplight held them in its small circle, two fists stacked one atop the other, and beyond the window the fog moved patient and grey.

Then Varga opened her hand.

The ring lay in her palm, dull in the lamplight, the heron standing on one leg with its beak pointed toward the window as if it meant to fly into the fog and never return. Varga did not lift her fingers to close around it again. She let her hand go flat on the blotter, the ring an island of silver in the sea of pale skin, and she looked at Wren with an expression that held no pride, no defeat—only the emptied quality of a ledger finally closed.

"Take it, then." Her voice was barely audible, a whisper that would not carry past the desk edge. "But understand what you are taking. Not the seal. Not the authority. You are taking the count. Seventy-six quarters. Eleven men. One pilot who swam to a boat in the dark and left the rest to drown. You are taking Maren's scarred hands and the weigher's careful capitals and the watchman who will look at you differently tomorrow because he will know, the way watchmen always know, that something has shifted in the building he patrols."

Wren looked at the ring. It was smaller than she had imagined, the heron no larger than her thumbnail, its single leg thinner than a nib. She thought of her own hands copying the Margery Lane entry three times, the careful stroke of the M, the curl of the y, the way her pen had moved across the page without knowing what it wrote.

"I understand," she said.

"No." Varga's free hand came up, not to stop her, but to point—one finger extended, the nail trimmed short, the gesture she used when correcting a column that did not balance. "You understand the words. The figures. The story I have told you. But you do not yet understand the weight, because the weight is not in the telling. The weight is in the next quarter, when the docket arrives and you must press the seal not because you believe in the arrangement but because you have become the arrangement. The weight is in the quarter after that, and the quarter after that, until you look up and discover that nineteen years have passed and you cannot remember whether you ever had a choice."

The stove ticked. The harbor bell rang, distant, counting an hour Wren had lost track of. She looked from the ring to Varga's face, and she saw what nineteen years had truly cost—not the grey beneath the eyes, not the tremor in the fingers, but the emptiness where belief had once lived, the hollow space of a woman who had balanced the account so perfectly that she had forgotten what the account was meant to measure.

"Give me the conditions," Wren said.

Varga's finger lowered. Her hand returned to the blotter, flat beside the fist that still held the ring between them.

"Three." The word was crisp now, the voice of the Master Assessor who had faced shipmasters and harbor inspectors and clerks who copied without seeing. "First: you seal the next docket yourself. No practice, no preparation. When the ship comes to berth nine tomorrow night, the docket will arrive at this desk, and you will press the heron into grey wax with your own hand, and you will enter the Margery Lane into the Salt Ledger in your own writing, and you will not flinch."

Wren nodded. The motion felt distant, as if someone else moved her head.

"Second: you never ask Maren what moves through berth nine. You never ask the weigher who writes the docket. You never ask who signs Cask's name. The arrangement lives because the left hand does not ask the right hand what it carries. If you break this, you break not only the secret but the trust that makes the secret possible, and the harbor will know within a week."

The fog pressed against the glass. Wren thought of Maren on the quay, the scarred hands, the quality of waiting that was not fear but something older.

"Third," Varga said, and her voice changed, dropping into the register she had used when telling of the man at the door, the seawater in his hair, the offer of balance in exchange for silence. "You carry the eleven men. Not as a story, not as a plaque on the harbor wall, not as a figure in a column that adds up. You carry them as your own. You do not speak their names aloud, because naming is not your right. But you do not forget them. You do not let the balance become so perfect that the dead disappear into the margin."

She stopped. Her hand moved at last, the fingers curling inward, the thumb pressing the heron ring against the heel of her palm. For a moment Wren thought she would close her fist again, that the offer would be withdrawn, that nineteen years of habit would prove stronger than the exhaustion that had opened her hand.

But Varga turned her palm upward, the ring resting in the center like a coin offered in payment, and she waited.

Wren reached for it.

The silver was cold against her skin, heavier than it looked, the heron's single leg pressing a faint line into her finger. She drew it from Varga's palm—not snatched, not stolen, but received, the way a clerk receives a docket from a weigher's hand, the transfer formal and complete. She held it up to the lamplight, and the heron caught the flame, and for a moment it seemed to move, the beak lifting, the leg tensing, a creature caught between flight and balance.

"It fits," Varga said, "on the middle finger of the right hand. The hand you use to hold the seal steady."

Wren slid it on. The silver warmed slowly, taking the temperature of her blood. It was loose—Varga's fingers were longer than hers—but not so loose that it would slip. She turned her hand, and the heron turned with it, and she understood that she had crossed a line that was not drawn in ink or wax but in the accumulated weight of seventy-six quarters, eleven men, and a woman who had finally found someone willing to ask why.

"The docket will arrive," Varga said, "when the tide turns tomorrow night. Until then, you have the ring, and you have the count, and you have—" She stopped. Her hands folded on the blotter, empty now, the pale circle where the ring had lived for nineteen years already fading from the skin. "You have what I had, Miss Adair. A ledger that balances. A secret that pays. And the first night of wondering whether you have bought something or sold something, and whether the difference matters."

The harbor bell rang again, closer now, counting the hour that belonged to neither night nor morning. Wren sat at her desk with the heron ring on her hand and understood that the story had ended in one way and begun in another. She was no longer the clerk who had discovered the anomaly. She was the anomaly. She was the seal that made the ghost ship real, the balance that kept the dead paying their taxes, the next chapter in a ledger that stretched back nineteen years and forward into a fog she could not yet see through.

She looked at Varga—really looked—and saw a woman emptied of her burden, diminished by the loss of it, smaller without the ring than she had been with it. The mentorship was over. The confession was complete. What remained was the work: the docket, the seal, the column that added up because someone had decided that adding up was enough.

Wren turned to a fresh page in the ledger she kept in her own mind. She began to write the first entry of her own accounting.
