## Chapter Text
Wren slid the docket between its neighbors and closed the box. The wood scraped softly against the shelf. She stood for a moment with her palm flat on the lid, feeling the grain, as if the box might tell her something the papers inside would not.

Then she reached for the one beside it.

The previous year's box was heavier, dark with tide-stains along the bottom. She lifted the lid and went through the quarters in order. The _Margery Lane_ docket was in each one, always near the bottom, always on the same cheap grey paper. _Copper ingot, forty hundredweight. Berth nine._ The weigher's capitals changed with the years — one hand cramped and careful, another loose and slanted, a third that looked like her own supervisor's — but the master's signature did not. It was always the same single looping _Cask_, as if one man had sat down in a quiet room and signed them all at once.

She kept going. The boxes grew lighter as the years retreated, the paper inside stiffening with salt. In the eighth year back she found the first pencilled message: _Tell O. the lamp is lit._ In the fifth year it was there again, in the third, the seventh — not every quarter, but often enough to be part of the arrangement. A signal. A code that had outlived the men who first wrote it down.

Nineteen years back, the first box. The wood was pale, barely stained, and when she opened it the dockets smelled of fresh ink and nothing else. The _Margery Lane_'s first entry sat at the top of the autumn quarter, not hidden at the bottom where later clerks had tucked it. The heron seal was pressed deep into grey wax, the bird's lines sharp enough to cut a finger. The cargo was the same. The berth was the same. The signature was the same.

Wren sat back on her heels. The cellar was cold, and her lamp was burning low. Seventy-six quarters, she counted. Seventy-six times a weigher had written those capitals, a clerk had copied them, an assessor had sealed them. Seventy-six times the harbor account had swallowed money for a ship that was already at the bottom of the Teeth.

She had done as Varga asked. The docket was back in its box, the box on its shelf, and the shelf held nineteen years of the same lie, told the same way, by hands that changed while the signature stayed still.

The water moved under the floor, restless, patient. Wren blew out her lamp and climbed the stairs in darkness, her fingers finding the gaps between the stones. The corridor above was empty. The watchman's bell had rung while she was below, and rung again, and she had not heard it.

She stepped out of the Customs House into a fog that had come in off the harbor while she worked. The plaque on the harbor wall was somewhere in that grey, eleven names she could not read from here. Tomorrow night the tide would bring the ship, or whatever moved under that dead name, into berth nine.

Wren stood on the step with the fog clinging to her coat. She had understanding now, wide and deep as the harbor itself, and nothing she could set on a desk to prove it.
