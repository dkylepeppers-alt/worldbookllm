## Chapter Text
Wren leaned forward. The desk edge pressed a line into her ribs, a mark she would find later and not remember receiving. "Tomorrow night's tide," she said. "The ship comes. You've told me how it began—Cask at your door, the triple tithe, the first seal. You've told me why you agreed. But you haven't told me what happens tomorrow."

Varga's hands were still flat on the blotter, the heron ring facing upward. She did not move them. "The ship comes," she said. "The lamp is lit. The tithe is paid. The ledger balances."

"That's not a plan. That's a habit."

The word struck. Wren saw it land—the slight flinch around Varga's eyes, the momentary tightening of her jaw. For nineteen years, no one had called the arrangement by its true name. It had been a system, a balance, a necessity. Never a habit. Never the small word that described what you did when you had forgotten why you started.

"Tell me," Wren said, and her voice had dropped to the register she used for checking columns, for finding errors, for insisting that the reason was written down somewhere if you were willing to look. "Tell me what ship comes to berth nine tomorrow night. Tell me what flag she flies, what cargo she carries, who meets her on the quay. Tell me whether Cask is alive or dead or a name that someone else signs. Tell me what you have been buying with seventy-six quarters of silence, and who you have been buying it from, and whether tomorrow night is an ending or just another seal in the wax."

Varga's mouth opened. Closed. The lamplight showed everything now—not the controlled mask of the Master Assessor, but the woman beneath, the one who had stood in a doorway nineteen years ago with a ring on her hand and a choice before her that she had never learned how to unmake.

"I don't know the ship," Varga said, and the admission cost her something visible—a settling in her shoulders, a release of tension that left her smaller than she had been. "I never have. The docket comes to my desk, I press the seal, the account balances. I don't go to the quay. I don't see the boat. I don't know if the man who signs 'Cask' is the same man who swam from the Teeth or his son or a clerk who learned the signature from a practice sheet."

She stopped. Her fingers curled inward, the heron ring disappearing into her palm.

"The cargo was copper once," she said. "Forty hundredweight, the same as the Margery Lane carried. That was the fiction—that the ship had survived, that the cargo still moved, that the harbor tithe was simply the harbor tithe on a working vessel. But that was nineteen years ago. I don't know what moves through berth nine now. I don't know if anything moves at all. The payments come. The seal is pressed. The balance holds."

Wren sat back. The chair creaked. She felt the room's warmth pressing against her fog-cooled skin, the too-hot stove and the lamplight and the fog beyond the glass, and understood that she had reached the center of the maze only to find it empty. Varga did not command the arrangement. She served it. The heron ring was not a mark of power but a mark of service, pressed into wax quarter after quarter because stopping would require a kind of courage that balance had slowly replaced.

"Then what do you want?" Wren asked. "From me. From tomorrow night. From the seventy-seventh quarter."

Varga looked at her. The lamplight caught the grey beneath her eyes, the fine lines, the slight tremor in the fingers that had held the ring for nineteen years. She did not answer immediately. The harbor bell rang, distant and slow, and the fog pressed against the window, and the two of them sat in the small circle of light while the tide turned beneath the pilings and the arrangement waited for its keeper to name what came next.
