## Chapter Text
She stayed where she was.

The cold found every gap in her clothing, seeping through wool and linen until her skin felt like the stone she crouched on. Her right hand had gone numb from clamping the roll of paper against her ribs. She shifted by fractions of an inch, her breath shallow and white in the fog that still clung to the lower harbor.

Maren stood by the lamp without moving. The fog made a statue of her, oilskins dark against the dark, the knitted cap pulled low. She checked the boat once more, her hands finding the lines by touch, and then she settled against the bollard with the patience of someone who had waited through worse nights than this.

The tide came in. Wren heard it in the rising voice of the water, the boat lifting on its lines until the fenders compressed and released against the quay with a rhythm like breathing. The fog began to thin toward the east, not enough to show the sky but enough to change the quality of the dark from black to grey. She had been crouched beside the crab traps for hours. Her knee had passed through pain into a dull absence that would punish her later, when she tried to stand.

A gull called, early and lonely, the sound that meant the fishing fleet would soon be stirring. Maren lifted her head at it, a small motion, and checked the lamp again. The wick was burning lower now, the oil dish half-empty, and Wren saw her frown—a slight tightening of the face, there and gone—and look toward the harbor mouth where the Teeth waited under the lightening dark.

Nothing came. The boat stayed ready, the lamp stayed lit, and the woman stayed watching. But the sky continued to pale, the grey becoming the particular colour of a Harrowmere dawn, salt-tinged and reluctant, and Wren understood that whatever the lamp summoned, it had not come this night.

Maren moved at last. She pinched out the flame with bare fingers, a quick motion that spoke of long practice, and coiled the painter with the efficiency of someone who had done this a thousand times. The boat was not left ready now; it was secured, covered, made ordinary. The signal had been given, and the signal had not been answered, and the day would begin as all the other days had begun, with the lamp dark and the ledger waiting and no one the wiser.

She walked past Wren's hiding place without looking, close enough that Wren smelled the oilskins and the harbor on her, close enough to touch. Her boots found the gaps between the cobbles with the same soundless knowledge she had shown coming in. Then she was gone, around the corner where the warehouse wall ended, back toward the fish market and the waking harbor and the life that did not include signal lamps on empty quays.

Wren waited until the footsteps faded. She waited longer, until the gulls were crying in earnest and a cart rumbled somewhere on the upper quay, the first movement of a town that had slept through her vigil. Then she stood, her knee buckling, her fingers numb and clumsy as she steadied herself against the warehouse wall.

The lamp sat on the stone, cold now, a simple dish of oil with a blackened wick. The boat rocked at its mooring, secured and ordinary. The fog was lifting in rags, showing her the shape of the north quay, the fish market awnings, the distant masts of the main harbor where ships that existed in the ledger as well as the water tied up and paid their proper tithes.

She had watched through the night and learned this much: the arrangement was tended. The lamp was lit and the lamp was extinguished, and someone had done both with the patience of nineteen years. The ship had not come, or had not chosen to come, or had seen something from the water that Wren could not see from the quay. But the mechanism lived. The lamp was part of it, and Maren was part of it, and the boat that waited ready and was then secured was part of it, and the whole thing turned on a rhythm older than Wren's questions.

She had the complete record in her coat. She had the face of the keeper in her memory. She had the knowledge of how the signal worked, what it meant, that it was a living practice and not a dead forgery. The fog had given her that, and the cold, and the hours of stillness while the harbor turned toward morning.

The tide was going out now. She could see the waterline on the quay stones, the kelp draped and drying, the marks that the night had left behind. Today the ship would come, Varga had said. Tomorrow night's tide. But the lamp had been lit tonight, and tended, and extinguished at dawn, and that meant the arrangement had its own calendar, its own reasons, its own patient accounting that did not match the quarters of the Salt Ledger.

Wren walked back along the north quay with the roll of paper heavy against her side and the morning light finding its way through the thinning fog. Her boots were wet through. Her hands were stiff with cold. She had not slept, and she would not sleep, because today was the day she would have to decide what to do with a truth that kept its own hours and lit its own lamps in the dark.
