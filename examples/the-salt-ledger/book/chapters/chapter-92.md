## Chapter Text
Wren went down the narrow stair at the end of the corridor, her hand finding the rope rail where the wall curved away into darkness. The fog had not reached this far, but the damp had—brine rising through the foundations, the cellar's eternal companion, turning paper to pulp and wood to something soft and yielding. Her boots found the familiar unevenness of the floor, the flagstone that dipped where the tide had pressed up through the mortar again and again, year after year.

The boxes stood in their rows, marked by year and quarter in a hand older than any clerk now living. She did not need the lamp she had left on the shelf; she knew the shape of this darkness, the particular quality of blackness that lived below the waterline of the Customs House. But she took the lamp anyway, the flame guttering as she lifted it, then steadying in the still air beneath the earth.

Nineteen years back. The first quarter after the sinking.

She found the box easily enough—the year was water-stained but legible, the quarter marked in faded ink. The wood had split along one corner, and she worked her fingers into the gap, prying gently, the way she had learned to handle swollen ledgers that would tear if rushed. Inside, the dockets lay in stacks, grey paper gone soft at the edges, some sheets stuck together by salt and time. She lifted them out one by one, reading the names by the lamplight, her fingers finding the raised lines of ink.

Harrow Belle. Salt Wife. The Teeth's Promise. Working ships, real ships, their dockets written in the weigher's careful capitals, signed by masters whose hands she did not know. She set each aside with the patience of her trade, the methodical sorting that had always been her gift and her refuge.

Then her fingers found it. Thicker paper, or perhaps only paper that had swollen differently, that had resisted the damp where the rest surrendered. The ship's name rose under her thumb like a scar: Margery Lane.

She carried it to the shelf and held it in the lamplight. The flame caught the paper and held it, yellow and wavering, and she read what the darkness had hidden.

Copper ingot, forty hundredweight. The same cargo as the autumn docket she had found hours before. The same berth: nine. The same looping signature, Cask, though she had never seen it before and could not have known to compare it. But the seal was different. Not the heron standing on one leg, worn soft by nineteen years of repetition. This seal was crisp, new, the wax still carrying the fine detail of a ring that had not yet grown tired of its lie. And the tithe amount—triple the usual rate, paid in advance for the quarter, a sum that would have balanced any ledger and silenced any question before it could form.

Wren held the docket in the lamplight and understood what Varga had not said upstairs, what her measured confession had circled without touching. This was not a debt assumed after tragedy. This was a purchase, made in advance, the very first quarter, before the ink could dry on the memorial plaque. The Margery Lane had not continued paying because someone felt guilty. It had continued because someone had bought its continued existence at a price that made refusal look like poor accounting.

The damp found her collar, a cold finger against her neck. She turned the docket over, searching for what the weigher might have added, what pencilled message might have preceded the one she knew. The back was blank, or nearly blank—a single mark, not words, not a signature. A smear of something that might have been wax, might have been water, might have been a thumb pressed hard enough to bruise the paper in grief or haste or the simple violence of sealing a bargain.

She turned it back. The flame moved, guttering in a draft that found the stair, and for a moment the signature Cask seemed to shift, the loop opening like a mouth, like a door, like the passage through the Teeth that had swallowed eleven men and let one through. Then the flame steadied, and it was only ink again, only a dead man's name written by a living hand.

The box waited behind her, its other dockets patient and undisturbed. The stair waited, leading up to Varga and the ledger room and the story that this document either confirmed or contradicted. And the tide waited, turning somewhere beyond the cellar walls, carrying tomorrow night's ship toward a harbor that had been bought and paid for nineteen years in advance.

Wren held the first docket in her hand, feeling its weight, the particular density of paper that had survived when so much else had softened into pulp. She had come to the cellar trusting documents over testimony. Now she held a document that made the testimony upstairs into something stranger than a lie—into a purchase, a bargain, a balance struck before the debt was even known.
