## Chapter Text
Wren pushed the roll deep into the cavity until her fingers grazed cold brick and the paper was lost to the dark. Seventy-six quarters, every clerk's name, her own slanted sevens repeated across nineteen years, the docket with its pencilled message nested inside like a seed. She straightened the wainscoting with her palm, brushing away the pale dust, and stepped back. The gap looked like any gap in an old building. The shelf above it held the same volumes in the same order. Nothing showed.

She capped her pen and put it in her pocket. The ledgers were closed, the desk bare, her lamp out. She stood in the dark for three breaths, listening to the harbor bell mark some fraction of the tide she had lost track of. Then she slid the bolt back and opened the door.

The corridor was empty. She walked with unhurried steps, the steps of a clerk who had stayed late to finish a reconciliation and was now going home to a cold supper. Her hands were empty. Her coat hung flat against her sides. If the watchman turned his lamp her way, he would see a junior clerk with ink on her fingers and nothing else.

The harbor door opened onto fog. It had come in while she worked, a grey tide thicker than the one that moved beneath the cellars. The lamps of the lower harbor were only smears of yellow, and the masts of the moored ships had lost their tops in the dark. Wren pulled her collar up and walked into it, her boots finding the cobbles by memory.

She had left the complete record behind. The thought came to her with each step, a rhythm underneath the harbor bell. Seventy-six quarters hidden in a wall, her own hand among them, the heron seal repeated like a pulse. If Varga went back to the ledger room—if she noticed the dust, the gap, the slight misalignment of a volume—she would find it all. Or if the watchman heard mice in the wall and called the porter. Or if the heating engineers returned after nineteen years to install new pipes. The reasons were written down, but they were no longer in Wren's hands.

She stopped at the corner where the main harbor met the north quay. The fog smelled of salt cod and cold stone. To her left the fish market was shuttered, the awnings furled and dripping; beyond it lay berth nine, where the Tidewardens kept their boats and the docket said the lamp was lit. To her right the larger ships tied up, their hulls creaking in the dark. Behind her, up the hill, Varga's rooms were above the chandlery, a single window that faced the harbor.

Her hands were empty. The complete record was in the wall, and the wall was in the Customs House, and the Customs House was behind her. She had preserved her deniability at the cost of her evidence. If someone stopped her now, she had nothing to show. If she needed to run, she could not take it with her. If she needed to bargain, she would have to go back.

The harbor bell began to count again, distant and muffled, measuring the tide that crept toward tomorrow night.
